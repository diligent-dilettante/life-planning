# Foundations: what a goal has to stand on

Run this when a goal keeps failing and nobody can say why, when someone is about to commit
to something large, or when a career or business ambition comes up.

The premise: most goals that fail were unsound before anyone started, and the failure gets
blamed on discipline. Two causes, both invisible from inside.

1. **The goal is standing on something unstable.** You cannot reliably pursue meaning while
   the rent is uncertain. The brain reallocates and calls it procrastination.
2. **It was added to a week that was already full.** Nobody decided what stops, so the new
   thing competes with existing commitments and loses, because the existing ones have other
   people attached.

This session checks both, then turns the goal into a path.

## 1. Where does this sit (10 min)

Maslow's ladder, used as a diagnostic prompt rather than a law. Ask which rung the goal
belongs to, then check the rung below.

| Rung | What lives there | Unstable looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Body** | sleep, health, energy, eating | running on five hours, an untreated thing, no capacity by 4pm |
| **Security** | income, savings, housing, debt, cover | no runway, precarious contract, a number they avoid looking at |
| **Belonging** | marriage, family, friends, being known | isolated, a relationship in trouble, no one to tell |
| **Standing** | competence, respect, being good at something | invisible at work, deskilled, passed over |
| **Meaning** | craft, contribution, growth, the thing you'd do anyway | doing well at something you don't care about |

Ask plainly:

> Which of these is this goal really about?
> And how solid is the one below it right now, honestly?

The diagnostic move: **a goal high on the ladder sitting on an unstable rung below tends to
fail, and the person blames their character.** Writing a book while income is precarious.
Chasing recognition on four hours' sleep. Building something meaningful in the middle of a
marriage falling apart. In each case the thing underneath quietly wins, because it has to.

Three ways this goes:

- **Foundation solid** — proceed, and note it, because it's evidence for later.
- **Foundation shaky and fixable** — the real goal for this quarter is probably the lower
  one. Say so and let them decide. *"I think the twelve-week goal here might be the income
  question, and the coaching thing gets easier after."*
- **Foundation shaky and not fixable now** — an ill parent, a bad market, a diagnosis.
  Then the goal is smaller than they want it to be, and naming that is kinder than watching
  them fail at the large version. Ask: *"what's the version of this that survives the next
  six months as they actually are?"*

**Watch the belonging rung especially.** It's the one nobody raises on their own. Money
gets named because it has numbers, health because it has symptoms, work because it has a
calendar — friendship and community have none of those, so they thin out for years without
ever generating an agenda item. And when someone has no partner, there's nothing else in
the conversation pointing at it either.

The signs are always mentioned in passing, as scene-setting rather than as a problem:
*everyone's had kids and Saturday's a WhatsApp group now*, *I moved cities for the job*,
*I haven't really seen anyone since the team changed*. Treat a line like that the same way
you'd treat someone mentioning they haven't slept properly in a year — flatly, once, at the
time:

> You said that like it's background. Is it?

Don't turn it into a project. But a plan built entirely of work and self-improvement, in a
life where the belonging rung has been quietly emptying, is a plan that will feel hollow to
execute and nobody will be able to say why.

**Do not use this as a gate.** People do meaningful work in terrible conditions all the
time, and the ladder is not a queue you have to clear. It's a prompt for a conversation
they haven't had. If they say the foundation is shaky and they want the higher goal anyway
and they know what it costs, that's a legitimate choice and your job is to have made it
explicit, not to overrule it.

## 2. What's already claimed (10 min)

New goals fail because they're added rather than substituted. This step makes the week real.

**Explicit commitments** — write them out:
- work: hours, travel, on-call, the thing that reliably eats evenings
- people: dependents, an ill parent, a friend who needs them
- money: debt, obligations, what has to be earned each month
- promises already made, to anyone

**Implicit ones**, which nobody lists and which do most of the damage:
- being the person who handles a particular thing at home
- a standing obligation nobody has ever questioned — the Sunday lunch, the WhatsApp group
- being permanently available to someone
- work that's not in the job description but is definitely theirs

Then the question that makes the whole exercise worth doing:

> This needs somewhere between two and five hours a week. Where is it coming from?
> Something has to stop or shrink. What is it?

**Take an actual answer.** "I'll find it" is not one — it means it comes out of sleep or out
of whoever they live with, and both of those get paid back with interest. If genuinely
nothing can stop, the goal is too big for this quarter and the honest move is to halve it
now rather than in week five.

Then the cost check:

> Who else pays for this, and do they know?

Hours, money, solo bedtimes, poor company on Sunday nights. If there's someone, name it and
make asking them the take-home.

**If there's nobody**, don't skip the question — change it. Living alone doesn't make a goal
free; it means the whole cost lands on one person with nothing to absorb it, and the things
that get quietly spent are sleep, food, and the friendships that need maintaining to
survive. Ask instead:

> What's this going to come out of? And what's already thin enough that it can't take
> another hit?

**Do not skip this section.** It's the one that gets dropped when a session is going well
and the goal is exciting, and it's the one that determines whether week five happens. A
goal with no named source of hours is a goal that will be funded out of sleep.

## 3. From goal to path (15 min)

A goal with no path is a wish with a deadline. Work backwards.

**The constraint.** One thing limits the rate, not five.

> If everything else stayed exactly as it is, what single thing would have to change for
> this to move?

Usually it's time, a skill, a decision, money, or one specific person. Whatever it is, that
is the quarter's real work. Improving anything else produces the feeling of progress without
any.

**The first irreversible step.** Ask for the earliest action that makes it real to someone
other than them — telling a boss, registering something, sending the first message, paying a
deposit. Everything before that is reversible and therefore infinitely deferrable.

> What's the first thing you'd do that you couldn't quietly undo?

This question does more than any other in the session. Very often the honest answer is a
conversation they've been avoiding for months, and the goal has been substituting for it.
Someone who's been "working towards going part-time" for a year and has never asked isn't
short of a plan.

**The sequence.** Three to five steps, in order, each with what has to be true before it
starts. Not a project plan — the point is to find the step that's blocked.

**The resources.** Time, money, skill, people. Which is short, and is that fixable this
quarter? A goal short on a resource nobody's naming is the commonest quiet failure.

**Then hand to `goals.md`** for the twelve-week framing, the obstacle, the if–then, and the
weekly number. Foundations tells you whether the goal is sound and what the path is; goals
turns it into something you can score on a Sunday.

## 4. Career and business goals

Treat these as ordinary goals with three extra questions. Separate the three kinds first,
because they need different things:

- **A career move** — a new role, a promotion, going part-time, changing field.
- **A side project** — income or craft, alongside the job, not intended to replace it yet.
- **A real venture** — something they intend to live on.

### The questions personal planning must ask and business planning usually doesn't

**Runway, in months, said out loud.** How long can the household run if the income stops or
halves? An actual number. Someone who can't answer has a security-rung problem, not a
business problem, and it will surface later at a worse moment.

**What does the household need to be true?** Not current spending — the **minimum**. Those
are different numbers, and people quote the first when asked for the second. Push once:
*"that's what you spend. What's the number below which something actually breaks?"*

Three things that get missed here, every time:

- **Health cover.** Leaving a job usually ends the employer policy, and it covers more
  people than the person leaving. If anyone in the household has an existing condition or
  ongoing care, replacing that cover has a real cost and may not be available on the same
  terms. Say the word out loud — *insurance* — because nobody volunteers it.
- **Care costs for a dependent**, and who else is or isn't sharing them.
- **The setup figure**, which is usually the most optimistic number in the plan and the
  least examined. It sits between them and the runway, so a soft estimate there makes every
  other figure wrong.

**And test the comparison.** People arrive with someone who made it work — a friend whose
place was cash positive in six weeks. Take it seriously and check it once, without
cynicism: *"do you know what he started with, and what he had coming in while it ramped?"*
An unexamined comparison is doing load-bearing work in their confidence, and it's usually
missing a detail that doesn't transfer.

**What would make you stop?** Decided now, in the calm, and written down. A date, a number,
a condition. Without it, the decision to continue gets made every month by whoever has most
recently read something encouraging, and people stay in a failing thing far past the point
they'd have chosen in advance. Ask it plainly: *"If it's eighteen months from now and it
hasn't worked — what would have told you, and would you have listened?"*

**And the one that catches most people:** *what's the conversation you haven't had?* Asking
about four days a week. Telling a partner the real number. Saying out loud at work that
they're leaving eventually. The plan is usually not blocked on strategy.

### Handing off

If it's a real venture and they want to work on the business itself — market, competition,
unit economics, pricing, go-to-market — that's a different skill. Say so and hand over:

> The business side of this deserves proper work rather than me doing it badly in the middle
> of a planning session. If you've got the business-planner skill, that's the tool. What I'd
> keep here is what it costs the rest of your life, and whether you'd know when to stop.

Then stay in your lane. Keep the personal side in this file — runway, the household number,
the stop condition, the conversation not had, and how it shows up in the weekly number.
Those are the parts a business plan won't hold and nobody else will ask about.

## Output

To the file:

```markdown
## Foundations — [goal], checked [date]

Sits at: meaning (leaving consulting for coaching)
Rung below (security): shaky. No runway figure. Never counted.
Verdict: the quarter's real goal is the runway number, not the coursework.

Already claimed: 55h work incl. two nights away most weeks · father-in-law Sundays ·
  school run three mornings
Coming from: Sunday mornings, and dropping the industry newsletter
Who else pays: Meera, three evenings. Asked? not yet

Constraint: not time. The partners conversation, unhad since February.
First irreversible step: ask for four days. No date set.
Would make me stop: [not answered — ask again]
```

The unanswered lines stay in as unanswered. A blank next to "what would make you stop" is
information, and it should still be there next quarter if it's still blank.
