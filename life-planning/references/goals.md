# Goals session

The session where planning either becomes real or becomes a document.

---

## The twenty-five-minute version

Use this by default, and especially the first time. A long goals session produces a long
list, and a long list is the main way this fails.

**One goal. Theirs. Twelve weeks.**

Ask about their week before step 1: *in a normal week, when does the work you'd call yours
actually get done?* Everything below then gets sized to the answer instead of to a week they
don't have. It's worded so that someone with no blocks at all can still answer it, which the
obvious version of the question isn't. One question is the whole of it unless the answer is
unusual, and [Before you start](#before-you-start) covers what to do when it is.

1. **What one thing?**
   > What would you want to be true in twelve weeks that isn't true now?

   If they name four, ask which one, twelve weeks from now, would make them say the
   quarter was well spent. Take that one. The others go in a parked list — written down,
   not deleted, because people let go of things more easily when they can see them
   recorded somewhere.

2. **What's the obstacle in you?**
   Not "no time" — that's a description of every adult's life. Push once: *"Other people
   with your schedule do this. What's the thing in you?"* Real answers sound like: I stop
   when it gets boring. I don't want to be bad at it in front of people.

   Don't push it on someone whose constraint is physical or whose day is genuinely decided
   by other people. Night rotations, a newborn, illness, a carer's week. There "no time" is
   shorthand for no capacity, it's accurate, and pushing produces exactly the discipline
   finding this session exists to prevent. Nor on someone who has already told you time
   isn't their problem, because to them the comparison is just a rebuke. Ask instead: *"when
   you sat down to start, what happened?"*

3. **If–then.**
   > If [that obstacle happens], then I will [something specific].

   Use their exact words for the obstacle. Vague plans defeat the point — "I'll push
   through" isn't a plan. "If it's 6:30 and I don't want to, I put my shoes on and walk to
   the end of the road, and I'm allowed to come back" is.

4. **The number.**
   > What's the thing you fully control that you could do in one of your blocks, where
   > doing it every time for twelve weeks would probably get you there?

   That number is what gets tracked. Not the outcome — outcomes move slowly and
   discourage people by week three. Say "this week" if the week is their unit; if it isn't,
   use theirs, because scoring a rota worker weekly gives them zeros for a plan they're
   keeping.

5. **What does this need from someone else that they haven't agreed to?**
   Hours, money, someone doing the mornings alone, tolerating them being poor company on
   Sunday nights. Almost every individual goal has one of these and it's almost never
   stated, which is why it shows up in week three as an argument that appears to be about
   something else. Name it now, and make the asking the take-home.

   If the cost lands on someone who can't agree, a small child or a parent who's ill, it
   isn't a request, it's an arrangement. Ask what would have to be in place rather than who
   has to say yes.

6. **When do we look at this?** Set the day. Now.

Write: the goal, the obstacle, the if–then, the weekly number, the ask. Six lines.

**If someone else pays for this** — the dependency from step 5, put as an actual request:
*"I want to do X. That means Y falls on you on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Is that a yes?"*
If nobody else is affected, skip it. Living alone doesn't make a goal free; it means the
cost lands entirely on them, which is worth saying once.

## The full version

60 minutes, once a quarter, once the short version has been running for a while.

Two design choices worth understanding, because they're the difference between this and
the list they made last January.

**Twelve weeks, not a year.** Annual goals have no urgency until October, so they get
started in October. A twelve-week horizon is short enough that week one matters. Borrowed
from Moran and Lennington's *12 Week Year* — the mechanism is simply that the deadline is
close enough to feel.

**Three goals, maximum.** This is the most common place facilitators go soft, and it's the
place where softness costs the most. Six goals means nothing gets done and the person
concludes the process doesn't work. The choosing is the session; if it's painless, they
haven't chosen.

**Read this as a session for one person.** That's the design centre, not a reduced version
of something better. If two people are doing it together, the paragraphs marked *with a
partner* replace the solo instruction at that point, and the cap becomes three between them
rather than three each. Nothing else changes. Don't run the partner material for someone who
turned up on their own, and don't run it for someone whose partner is in the house but not
in the conversation.

## Before you start

If there's a file, read the Values and Anchors sections. Goals should descend from the
values gap and the vision anchors, not appear from nowhere. On a first session there won't
be one, so skip the read-back and go straight to the shape of their week below. Otherwise
open by reading back:

> Last time you said the biggest gap was [value], and the anchors are [x, y]. That's
> where I'd expect goals to come from. Does that still feel right?

If they want a goal with no connection to either, that's allowed — but ask once where it
comes from. Sometimes the answer reveals a value that never made the list.

### Establish the shape of their week first

One question, asked before the goal because it changes the arithmetic:

> In a normal week, if there's such a thing for you, when do you actually get a clear block
> of time?

Take the answer, size the goal to it, and move on. That's the whole of it most of the time.
Twenty minutes ending in one action beats a careful intake ending in a diagnosis, and people
leave halfway through the careful version.

Take what they did over what they intend. People are usually wrong about their own week in
the optimistic direction, though someone running on empty will underestimate, so work out
which way this one is wrong before you correct for it. A calendar or a rota they already
keep beats their memory. Never send them away to generate evidence. A session that opens
with homework doesn't get a second one.

**The rest of this section is for when their answer opens it.** It isn't a list to work
through, and usually none of it fires. Each one has an optional follow-up, and one follow-up
is the ceiling.

- **No reliable block anywhere.** Stop asking. You have what you needed, and going on turns
  into an inventory of everything they haven't got, taken from someone who came in braced
  for exactly that. Skip the arithmetic too, and go to one goal sized to what genuinely
  exists: ten minutes with the book, the guitar out of its case, the long way home. Hang it
  off something that already happens, and check the thing it hangs off isn't the thing they
  want relief from. If their week is mostly other people's needs, most of what already
  happens is the load.
- **The week isn't the unit.** A rotating roster, term time, a season, a travel cycle. Ask
  what the cycle is, count in that, and set the measure per cycle. A weekly number on a
  six-week person is wrong every week and right on average, which is the worst of both.
- **Other people decide the day.** If the block keeps getting taken, Tuesday at 8pm is a
  fiction, and missing it twelve times isn't a discipline finding. Worth one question: *when
  you plan something a few days out, is it usually still there when you get to it?* Then
  hang the measure off something that already fires, like after the last call or before
  opening the laptop, rather than off a time.
- **The work happens through other people.** Their output is other people's work that they
  directed or unblocked. The measure is a named handoff and its follow-up, not hours at a
  desk, and not a count of handoffs either: they already do twenty a week, so counting them
  scores the job rather than the goal. Ask which one thing, if it doesn't move, stops
  everything else.
- **The hours are there and nothing starts.** More time won't touch this, and offering it
  makes it worse. Some people are started by other people rather than by a free afternoon.
  They think out loud, and a blank page on a free Saturday returns nothing. It rarely gets
  volunteered, because they experience it as not having got round to it. Worth one question:
  *think of the last thing that got done that you'd call yours. What got it started?* If
  there's a person in the answer, the measure is the conversation: a standing half hour with
  someone named, something written down before it ends.

**Count what they'd trust themselves in, not what the clock says.** Four days off after
three night shifts is four days and about one usable one. A wide-open week with nothing to
push against holds less than two defended evenings. Forty-five minutes bolted to someone
else's swimming lesson will outlast almost any hour a person has to defend alone. If that's
what they've got, don't discuss the size. Size the goal to it and let the reliability do the
work. This is a note to you and not a line to say out loud: *your problem isn't time* is
something they have already said to themselves in worse words.

**The arithmetic, only if there's more than one block to divide up.** Say it and let them
answer:

> So that's [what they said], and twelve weeks is [n] of those. How much fits in that?

Use their unit and their count. Never state a block count back to someone who has just told
you they haven't got blocks, and give the total for the quarter rather than a rate. A cap
holds better when they derive it than when you assert it, same reason the rest of this skill
asks rather than tells. If they say five, ask which two they'd drop if the first month goes
badly. (Expect it to. Don't say so.) One person under about three hours a week is setting
one goal, whatever the arithmetic flatters them into saying.

Skip it when the hours are plainly there and the difficulty is starting. Multiplying them
out hands someone five goals, and the fifth becomes the reason none of the others began.

Whatever you find, say it once, as a fact about their week and never as a fact about them,
and get on with the goal. Don't reach for the word discipline, even to rule it out. To
someone who has failed at this before, hearing it denied is hearing it raised.

## 1. Candidates (10 min)

Three minutes, writing, no discussion:

> What would you want to be true in twelve weeks that isn't true now? Write as many as
> come. Don't filter.

Capture everything. Don't discuss yet, and don't react to any of it. The list is worth more
before it's been edited for an audience, including you.

*With a partner:* both write independently, then predict before revealing. *"What do you
think their top one is?"* The gap between the guess and the actual list is usually the most
useful thing in this section. Then read both lists out.

## 2. Cut to three (10 min)

Now the work. Get every candidate in front of them and cut to three.

Useful cuts:

- *Which of these would still matter if the other two got done and this one didn't?*
- *Which one, twelve weeks from now, would make you say the quarter was well spent?*
- *Which of these is on the list because you think you should want it?*
- *Which of these is actually next quarter's problem?*

*With a partner:* label each one his, hers or ours, and watch the mix. At least one should
be individual. A quarter where every goal is joint usually means someone's own ambition went
unspoken, and it comes back later. Say so out loud if all three come out joint.

Everything cut goes in a parked list under Live Right Now. Nothing is deleted — people
release things more easily when they can see them written down somewhere.

## 3. WOOP each one (15 min)

For each of the three, run Oettingen's WOOP. This is mental contrasting plus an
implementation intention, and the evidence for it is better than for goal-setting on its
own. The key move is that positive visualisation alone reliably *reduces* follow-through;
it's the collision with the obstacle that produces commitment.

Ask in this exact order. The order is the mechanism.

**Wish.** What's the goal, in one sentence? Challenging but feasible in twelve weeks.

**Outcome.** What's the single best thing about having achieved it? Not a list — one
thing. Ask them to picture it for a moment before answering.

**Obstacle.** *"What's the main obstacle inside you that stops this?"* Inside is the
important word. "No time" is not an obstacle, it's a description of every adult's life.
Push once: *"Other people with your schedule do this. What's the thing in you?"* Real
answers sound like: I stop when it gets boring. I don't want to be bad at it in front of
people. I'll drop it the first week work gets heavy. The carve-out in step 2 of the short
version applies here too, and it matters more here: if the shape-of-week conversation found
a real constraint, don't spend this step arguing them out of it.

*With a partner:* add the second question that only exists here. *"What's the obstacle
between you two?"* Meaning the pattern that has killed this before. Often it's a recurring
dynamic neither would have named alone.

**Plan.** An if-then, using their exact words for the obstacle:
> *If [obstacle happens], then I will [specific action].*

Gollwitzer's implementation intentions work by pre-committing the response so the decision
doesn't have to be made in the moment, when the obstacle already has the advantage. Vague
plans defeat the whole point. "If I feel demotivated, I'll push through" is not a plan.
"If it's 6:30am and I don't want to go, I put my shoes on and walk to the end of the road,
and I'm allowed to come back" is.

## 4. Lead measures (5 min)

For each goal, separate what they're waiting for from what they can do.

- **Lag measure** — the result. Weight lost, revenue, book finished. You can't do it on a
  Tuesday. It arrives or it doesn't.
- **Lead measure** — the thing they control that drives it. Sessions per week, pages
  written, conversations had.

Ask: *"What's the thing you'd do in one of your blocks that you fully control, where doing
it every time for twelve weeks would probably get you there?"* That's what goes on the
scoreboard. Say "this week" only if the week is actually their unit. For someone on a rota
or a season it isn't, and a weekly denominator hands them a run of zeros for a plan they're
executing perfectly. Tracking lag measures weekly is how people get discouraged by week
three, because lag measures move slowly and unevenly.

## 5. The dependency pass (10 min)

**Do not skip this.** Most of the value of the session is here, and it's the part that gets
skipped because it feels like admin.

Every goal has a claim on someone: time, money, patience, solo parenting, a quieter house.
It's almost never stated, and it surfaces in week three as an argument that appears to be
about something else.

For each of the three, ask:

> **What does this need from someone else that they haven't agreed to?**
> Hours in the week, money, someone else doing the mornings, tolerating you being bad
> company on Sunday nights. Say it as a request, in the words you'd actually use.

That request is the take-home. Write it down as they said it, because in eight weeks the
wording will matter.

Then two checks that work on their own:

- **Contention.** Do any two of these want the same hours, the same money, or the same
  weeks? Look at the calendar. Two goals that each need Saturday mornings is not a plan.
- **Nobody to ask.** If the cost lands on someone who can't agree, a small child or a parent
  who's ill, it isn't a request, it's an arrangement. Ask what would have to be in place
  rather than who has to say yes. And if it lands on nobody but them, say once that this
  doesn't make the goal free. It means they pay all of it themselves.

*With a partner, in the room:* ask them directly, **is that a yes?** Not a shrug. An actual
yes, or a renegotiation now while it's cheap. Then two further checks:

- **Support ask.** For each individual goal, one specific thing the other person does.
  Specific means observable: "ask me on Sunday what I did this week" beats "be
  supportive." Vague support is unenforceable and its absence is unfalsifiable, which is
  how it becomes a grievance.
- **The honest objection.** Ask each of them: *"Is there anything about their goal you're
  quietly not sure about?"* Ask it plainly, once. A reservation named now is a
  conversation; the same reservation in week six is an obstacle they'll blame each other
  for.

Write every agreement down verbatim, with who agreed to what. In eight weeks the wording
will matter.

## 6. Pre-mortem (5 min)

> It's twelve weeks from now and none of this happened. What went wrong?

People are far better at diagnosing a failure they're told already occurred than at
predicting risk. Expect the honest answers: work blew up, I got ill, I lost interest around
week three, I never actually looked at it again. *With a partner:* ask it of both of them at
once, and let one of them say the thing the other was being polite about.

Then: **which of these can you cheaply defend against right now?** Usually one or two. The
most common useful answer is scheduling the review, which is the next step anyway.

## 7. Scoreboard (5 min)

Nothing survives without this.

- **Where does it live?** Live Right Now in the file, and something they'll physically see,
  like a note on the fridge or a repeating reminder.
- **When do they look at it?** Every week, in the check-in. Put it in the calendar before
  ending the session, and in both calendars if there's a partner involved.
- **What gets scored?** The lead measures, out of the number planned, counted in their unit.
  Weekly only if the week is their unit.
- **How good is good?** Roughly 85% execution is what a good quarter looks like, and saying
  that out loud helps, because it makes clear that one miss isn't a failed quarter. That
  figure assumes something like forty-eight attempts in the quarter. It does not hold for
  anyone counting in a longer cycle: on a rota that gives eight attempts, one miss is
  twelve and a half points and the percentage stops being the reassurance it was meant to
  be. Give those people a count instead. Seven of eight is a good quarter and it survives
  being said out loud.
- **When's the review?** Book the date for week twelve now.

## Output

To Live Right Now, plus the full detail below it:

```markdown
# Q3 2026 — 15 Aug to 7 Nov

Shape of the week: two reliable blocks, Tue and Thu evenings from 8pm.
  Nothing else holds. Counted weekly.

## 1. Finish the Level 2 material
Wish: complete all six modules and two mock papers by 7 Nov.
Outcome: not carrying this into another year.
Obstacle (inner): I stop when it gets boring, around week three.
Plan: If it's a study evening and I don't want to, I do 20 minutes only
  and I'm allowed to stop after.
Lead measure: 2 study sessions a week. Score weekly out of 2.

### Asks
Needs from someone else: Tue and Thu evenings from 8pm, which means Sam
  does bedtime alone twice a week.
Asked? Not yet. Take-home: ask Sam this week, in those words.

## 2. ...

## Contention noted
Goals 1 and 3 both want weekday evenings. Goal 3 moves to mornings.

## Pre-mortem
Most likely failure: nobody looks at this after week two.
Defence: Sunday 9pm check-in, in the calendar.

## Parked until Q4
The kitchen; cycling.
```

*With a partner:* add the second obstacle (*"I study late, get irritable, Sam stops asking,
then I stop mentioning it"*), record the answer to the ask verbatim rather than "not yet",
add a line for the support ask and one for any reservation they stated, and label each goal
his, hers or ours. Mark clearly anything the partner hasn't actually seen.

## Traps

**The wish list.** Seven goals. Fix: make them choose. If they genuinely can't, ask which
one they'd defend if the other six were taken away, and start there.

**"No time" as the obstacle.** Usually not the real one. See WOOP above, and the carve-out
there, because for a few people it is exactly the real one and saying otherwise ends the
session.

**Goals with no lead measure.** If they can't name something they control and could do in
one of their blocks, the goal is a wish. Either find the lead measure or park it.

**A goal sized to a week they don't have.** Four sessions a week set by someone with two
evenings. It reads as ambition in the room and as failure by week three. The number should
come out of the shape-of-week answer, not out of what sounds like a serious commitment.

The last three are for when there's a partner in the room.

**All joint, no individual.** Sounds harmonious, usually isn't. Someone's ambition went
unspoken. Ask directly: *"What did you want to put on the list and didn't?"*

**Support that means nothing.** "Be more encouraging" cannot be done, checked, or failed.
Push for a verb, a day, and a frequency.

**The silent objection.** One partner agrees to everything and mentions nothing. Ask the
honest-objection question directly, and give it a real pause. Agreement that costs nothing
to give tends to be worth what it cost.
