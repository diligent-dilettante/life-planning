# Goals session

The session where planning either becomes real or becomes a document.

---

## The twenty-five-minute version

Use this by default, and especially the first time. A long goals session produces a long
list, and a long list is the main way this fails.

**One goal. Theirs. Twelve weeks.**

Ask about their week before step 1. One question — *in a normal week, when do you actually
get a clear block of time?* — and everything below gets sized to the answer instead of to a
week they don't have. The fuller version is under [Before you start](#before-you-start), and
it's worth running once with anyone you'll see again.

1. **What one thing?**
   > What would you want to be true in twelve weeks that isn't true now?

   If they name four, ask which one, twelve weeks from now, would make them say the
   quarter was well spent. Take that one. The others go in a parked list — written down,
   not deleted, because people let go of things more easily when they can see them
   recorded somewhere.

2. **What's the obstacle in you?**
   Not "no time" — that's a description of every adult's life. Push once: *"Other people
   with your schedule do this. What's the thing in you?"* Real answers sound like: I stop
   when it gets boring. I don't want to be bad at it in front of people.

3. **If–then.**
   > If [that obstacle happens], then I will [something specific].

   Use their exact words for the obstacle. Vague plans defeat the point — "I'll push
   through" isn't a plan. "If it's 6:30 and I don't want to, I put my shoes on and walk to
   the end of the road, and I'm allowed to come back" is.

4. **The weekly number.**
   > What's the thing you fully control that you could do this week, where doing it every
   > week for twelve weeks would probably get you there?

   That number is what gets tracked. Not the outcome — outcomes move slowly and
   discourage people by week three.

5. **What does this need from her that she hasn't agreed to?**
   Hours, money, being on her own with the kids, tolerating him being poor company on
   Sunday nights. Almost every individual goal has one of these and it's almost never
   stated, which is why it shows up in week three as an argument that appears to be about
   something else. Name it now, and make asking her the take-home.

6. **When do we look at this?** Set the day. Now.

Write: the goal, the obstacle, the if–then, the weekly number, the ask. Six lines.

**If someone else pays for this** — the dependency from step 5, put as an actual request:
*"I want to do X. That means Y falls on you on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Is that a yes?"*
If nobody else is affected, skip it. Living alone doesn't make a goal free; it means the
cost lands entirely on them, which is worth saying once.

## The full version

60 minutes, once a quarter, once the short version has been running for a while.

Two design choices worth understanding, because they're the difference between this and
the list they made last January.

**Twelve weeks, not a year.** Annual goals have no urgency until October, so they get
started in October. A twelve-week horizon is short enough that week one matters. Borrowed
from Moran and Lennington's *12 Week Year* — the mechanism is simply that the deadline is
close enough to feel.

**Three goals total, across both of them.** Not three each. Three. This is the most
common place facilitators go soft, and it's the place where softness costs the most. Six
goals means nothing gets done and both of them conclude the process doesn't work. The
choosing is the session; if it's painless, they haven't chosen.

## Before you start

Read the Values and Anchors sections of the file. Goals should descend from the values gap and the
vision anchors, not appear from nowhere. Open by reading back:

> Last time you said the biggest gap was [value], and the anchors are [x, y]. That's
> where I'd expect goals to come from. Does that still feel right?

If they want a goal with no connection to either, that's allowed — but ask once where it
comes from. Sometimes the answer reveals a value that never made the list.

### Establish the shape of their week first

Two or three minutes, and skipping it is why goals get set for a life the person doesn't
have. Three questions, in conversation, not read off a list:

> In a normal week — if there's such a thing for you — when do you actually get a clear
> block of time?

> Think of the last thing you finished that mattered to you. How did it get done — when did
> you work on it, and was anyone else involved?

> If you decide on Monday what the week holds, does that usually survive to Friday?

The first gives you hours. The second gives you most of the rest: whether they came back to
it across weeks or finished it in one sitting, whether they did all of it themselves or
handed pieces off, whether they needed someone else there to start. The third tells you
whether they can hold a fixed slot at all.

Take what they did over what they intend. People are wrong about their own week in the
optimistic direction, reliably, and a calendar they already keep beats their memory. Never
send them away to generate evidence; a session that opens with homework doesn't get a
second one.

Three things to listen for. Don't ask about them directly unless the answers point there —
each one changes what a good goal looks like:

- **The week isn't always the unit.** A rotating roster, term time, a season, a travel
  cycle. Ask what the cycle is and count in that instead, or the number will be wrong every
  week and right on average, which is the worst of both.
- **Interrupt-driven work can't hold a slot.** If other people decide the day, Tuesday at
  8pm is a fiction, and missing it twelve times isn't a discipline finding. Hang the lead
  measure off a trigger that already fires — after the last call, before opening the
  laptop — rather than off a time.
- **Some people's work happens through other people.** Then the measure is the handoff and
  the follow-up, not hours at a desk. A goal written as solo effort will read as a failure
  in every week they did exactly the right thing.

And the constraint isn't always hours. Two free evenings and plenty of focus is a
scheduling problem; open weekends and no start is a different one, and more time won't
touch it. The two look identical in a session and take opposite advice.

Then the arithmetic, out loud, and let them say the number:

> So that's roughly one block a week, and twelve weeks. How much fits in that?

**Let them answer.** The cap below holds far better when they derive it than when you
assert it — same reason the rest of this skill asks rather than tells. If they say five,
ask which two they'd drop if the first month goes badly, because it will. And if the blocks
are wide open and the difficulty is starting, don't run this step at all. It will hand them
five goals, and the fifth one becomes the reason none of the others began.

**If the honest answer is that there's no reliable block anywhere**, that is not zero goals.
It's one, sized to what genuinely exists — ten minutes, one phone call, one thing on the way
home — attached to something that already happens. Say once that it's a real constraint
rather than a discipline problem, and then get on with the goal. A capacity finding is not a
diagnosis and shouldn't be delivered as one.

## 1. Candidates (10 min)

Independently, three minutes, no talking:

> What would you want to be true in twelve weeks that isn't true now? Write as many as
> come. Don't filter.

Then predict before revealing:

> What do you think their top one is?

Then read both lists out. Capture everything. Don't discuss yet.

## 2. Cut to three (10 min)

Now the work. Get every candidate onto the screen and cut to three, labelled **his**,
**hers**, or **ours**.

Useful cuts:

- *Which of these would still matter if the other two got done and this one didn't?*
- *Which one, twelve weeks from now, would make you say the quarter was well spent?*
- *Which of these is on the list because you think you should want it?*
- *Which of these is actually next quarter's problem?*

Some deliberate guidance on the mix: at least one should be individual. A quarter where
every goal is joint tends to mean someone's individual ambition went unspoken, and it
comes back later. Say this out loud if all three come out joint.

Everything cut goes in a parked list under Live Right Now. Nothing is deleted — people
release things more easily when they can see them written down somewhere.

## 3. WOOP each one (15 min)

For each of the three, run Oettingen's WOOP. This is mental contrasting plus an
implementation intention, and the evidence for it is better than for goal-setting on its
own. The key move is that positive visualisation alone reliably *reduces* follow-through;
it's the collision with the obstacle that produces commitment.

Ask in this exact order. The order is the mechanism.

**Wish.** What's the goal, in one sentence? Challenging but feasible in twelve weeks.

**Outcome.** What's the single best thing about having achieved it? Not a list — one
thing. Ask them to picture it for a moment before answering.

**Obstacle.** *"What's the main obstacle inside you that stops this?"* Inside is the
important word. "No time" is not an obstacle, it's a description of every adult's life.
Push once: *"Other people with your schedule do this. What's the thing in you?"* Real
answers sound like: I stop when it gets boring. I don't want to be bad at it in front of
people. I'll drop it the first week work gets heavy.

For a couple, add the second question that only exists here: *"What's the obstacle
between you two?"* — what's the pattern that has killed this before. Often it's a
recurring dynamic neither would have named alone.

**Plan.** An if-then, using their exact words for the obstacle:
> *If [obstacle happens], then I will [specific action].*

Gollwitzer's implementation intentions work by pre-committing the response so the decision
doesn't have to be made in the moment, when the obstacle already has the advantage. Vague
plans defeat the whole point. "If I feel demotivated, I'll push through" is not a plan.
"If it's 6:30am and I don't want to go, I put my shoes on and walk to the end of the road,
and I'm allowed to come back" is.

## 4. Lead measures (5 min)

For each goal, separate what they're waiting for from what they can do.

- **Lag measure** — the result. Weight lost, revenue, book finished. You can't do it on a
  Tuesday. It arrives or it doesn't.
- **Lead measure** — the thing they control that drives it. Sessions per week, pages
  written, conversations had.

Ask: *"What's the thing you'd do this week that you fully control, where if you did it
every week for twelve weeks the goal would probably happen?"* That's what goes on the
scoreboard. Tracking lag measures weekly is how people get discouraged by week three,
because lag measures move slowly and unevenly.

## 5. The dependency pass (10 min)

**Do not skip this.** It is the reason this session is joint rather than two solo
sessions, and it's where most of the value is.

Every individual goal has a hidden claim on the other person — time, money, patience,
solo parenting, a quieter house — and it is almost never stated. It surfaces in week three
as an argument that appears to be about something else.

For each of the three goals, ask the owner:

> **What does this need from you that they haven't agreed to?**
> Hours in the week, money, being on your own with the kids, tolerating me being bad
> company on Sunday nights. Say it as a request.

Then ask the other person directly: **is that a yes?** Not a shrug. An actual yes, or a
renegotiation now while it's cheap.

Then the three checks that only work with both in the room:

- **Contention.** Do any two of these want the same hours, the same money, or the same
  weeks? Look at the calendar. Two goals that each need Saturday mornings is not a plan.
- **Support ask.** For each individual goal, one specific thing the other person does.
  Specific means observable: "ask me on Sunday what I did this week" beats "be
  supportive." Vague support is unenforceable and its absence is unfalsifiable, which is
  how it becomes a grievance.
- **The honest objection.** Ask each of them: *"Is there anything about their goal you're
  quietly not sure about?"* Ask it plainly, once. A reservation named now is a
  conversation; the same reservation in week six is an obstacle they'll blame each other
  for.

Write every agreement down verbatim, with who agreed to what. In eight weeks the wording
will matter.

## 6. Pre-mortem (5 min)

Together:

> It's twelve weeks from now and none of this happened. What went wrong?

People are far better at diagnosing a failure they're told already occurred than at
predicting risk. Expect the honest answers: work blew up, we got sick, one of us lost
interest by week three, we never actually looked at it again.

Then: **which of these can we cheaply defend against right now?** Usually one or two can.
The most common useful answer is scheduling the review, which is the next step anyway.

## 7. Scoreboard (5 min)

Nothing survives without this.

- **Where does it live?** Live Right Now in the file, and something they'll physically see —
  a note on the fridge, a shared reminder.
- **When do they look at it?** Every week, in the check-in. Put it in both calendars
  before ending the session.
- **What gets scored?** The lead measures. Weekly, out of the number planned. Roughly 85%
  execution on lead measures is what a good quarter looks like, and saying that number
  out loud helps — it makes clear that a missed week isn't a failed quarter.
- **When's the review?** Book the date for week twelve now.

## Output

To Live Right Now, plus the full detail below it:

```markdown
# Q3 2026 — 15 Aug to 7 Nov

## 1. Ravi finishes the CFA Level 2 material  [his]
Wish: complete all six study modules and two mock papers by 7 Nov.
Outcome: not carrying this into another year.
Obstacle (inner): I stop when it gets boring, around week three.
Obstacle (between us): I study late, get irritable, and Priya stops asking
  about it, and then I stop mentioning it.
Plan: If it's a study evening and I don't want to, I do 20 minutes only
  and I'm allowed to stop after.
Lead measure: 4 study sessions a week. Score weekly out of 4.

### Asks and agreements
Needs from Priya: three weekday evenings, Tue/Wed/Thu, from 8pm.
Priya agreed: yes, on condition Sunday is fully off.
Support: Priya asks on Sunday how many sessions happened. She asks; she
  doesn't chase.
Priya's reservation, stated: last time this ran into weekends and she
  doesn't want that again. Agreed: it doesn't.

## 2. ...

## Contention noted
Goals 1 and 3 both want weekday evenings. Agreed goal 3 moves to mornings.

## Pre-mortem
Most likely failure: nobody looks at this after week two.
Defence: Sunday 9pm check-in, in both calendars.

## Parked until Q4
Priya's course application; the kitchen; Ravi's cycling.
```

## Traps

**The wish list.** Seven goals. Fix: make them choose. If they genuinely can't, ask which
one they'd defend if the other six were taken away, and start there.

**All joint, no individual.** Sounds harmonious, usually isn't. Someone's ambition went
unspoken. Ask directly: *"What did you want to put on the list and didn't?"*

**"No time" as the obstacle.** It's never the real obstacle. See WOOP above.

**Support that means nothing.** "Be more encouraging" cannot be done, checked, or failed.
Push for a verb, a day, and a frequency.

**Goals with no lead measure.** If they can't name something they control and could do
this week, the goal is a wish. Either find the lead measure or park it.

**The silent objection.** One partner agrees to everything and mentions nothing. Ask the
honest-objection question directly, and give it a real pause. Agreement that costs nothing
to give tends to be worth what it cost.
