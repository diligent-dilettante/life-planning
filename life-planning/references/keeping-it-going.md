# Keeping it going

The part that actually determines whether any of this was worth doing.

Read this before running a goals session or setting up check-ins. If someone tells you
they've planned before and it didn't stick, start here rather than making another plan —
making plans is the thing they already know how to do.

## Why it dies

Six causes, roughly in order of how often they're the real one. Diagnose before
prescribing — the fix is different for each, and prescribing the wrong one is how a couple
concludes that nothing works for them.

**1. Too much.** Six goals, a tracking spreadsheet, three session types, a values document.
The system takes more energy than the goals do. Effort spent on the apparatus is effort not
spent on the thing. *Fix: cut to one goal each and one weekly question. Ruthlessly. You can
add later; you cannot recover credibility once the system feels like homework.*

**2. Nothing triggers a look.** The plan is in a file nobody opens. No calendar entry, no
fixed slot, no prompt. Nobody decided not to look at it — the looking just never had a
cause. *Fix: a recurring slot attached to something that already happens reliably. Sunday
evening, after the kids are down, whatever their week actually contains.*

**3. The first miss becomes the verdict.** Week three goes badly. The miss gets read as
evidence the plan was naive, and the natural response to a plan you now think was naive is
to stop. This is the largest single cause, and it's almost entirely a framing problem.
*Fix: say before it happens that the first miss is coming and is not information about
whether this works. Then have a restart move ready, because it's the restart that's
missing, not the discipline.*

**4. One person was never really in it.** One enthusiastic, one agreeable. It runs on the
enthusiastic one's energy until that runs out, usually around week five. *Fix: catch it in
the intake. Ask the quieter one directly what they'd want out of it, and if the honest
answer is "not much", build something much smaller that serves the one who wants it,
rather than a joint programme that will fail and be read as a relationship problem.*

**5. The plan is separate from the life.** Goals live in a document; the actual week is
run by email and school pickups. Nothing connects them. *Fix: lead measures that attach to
existing routines, and one item on the plan that shows up in the physical world — a
recurring calendar block, a note on the fridge.*

**6. It became an argument.** One session went badly and now the meeting has a bad
association. *Fix: acknowledge it directly, shorten the format, and put the hard topic
somewhere else. See `stuck.md`.*

## The twenty-minute restart

When they've fallen off — and they will, more than once — this is the whole conversation.
It's short on purpose. A long post-mortem about why you stopped is a good way to not
restart today.

**How this squares with the intake.** The intake in `SKILL.md` asks what happened last
time and digs a little. That's for someone arriving fresh after a long gap, where the
previous attempt is the whole reason they're here and diagnosing it is the work. The
restart below is for a lapse inside a cycle that's otherwise running — three missed weeks,
not eleven months. Diagnose once, at the start of an engagement. After that, when they slip
and come back, get them moving rather than re-opening the enquiry every time. Repeatedly
analysing the same lapse is its own way of not restarting.

1. **No autopsy.** Ask once, lightly: *"What got in the way?"* Take the first answer. Do
   not dig. The goal is to be running again by the end of this conversation, not to
   understand the lapse fully.
2. **What's still true?** Read the old goals back. Usually one or two still matter and the
   rest have quietly stopped mattering. That's not failure, that's information — the list
   was too long and reality has now edited it for them. Say that.
3. **Drop most of it.** Keep one thing each, at most. Explicitly delete the rest, out loud,
   so it stops being a debt.
4. **Halve it.** Whatever the lead measure was, halve it. Four sessions a week becomes two.
   The aim is a version so small it's embarrassing to miss, because what they need to
   rebuild is the belief that they do what they said, not the outcome.
5. **When's the next look?** Set it now, in the calendar, before ending.

Twenty minutes. Then stop talking about it.

## Designing accountability that survives a bad week

Most accountability designs assume a good week. The design only matters in a bad one.

**One number, one question, one week.** For each live goal, a single number they can say
in five seconds. "Three of four." Not a reflection, not a rating, not a paragraph. If
answering takes longer than a minute, it won't get answered in the week where it counts.

**Ask, don't chase.** The agreed move between partners is *asking*, once, at the agreed
time. Chasing turns a partner into a supervisor, and the reliable response to a supervisor
is avoidance. Get the wording agreed in the room — "how many did you get to?" is fine;
tone matters more than words and both of them know it.

**Make it observable, not evaluative.** "Did you do the four sessions?" is answerable.
"How's it going?" invites a story, and the story is where the slippage hides.

**Nothing depends on their diligence.** No tracker they have to maintain, no habit-marking
app, no weekly form. Every system that requires the user to be organised fails the person
who most needs it. See below.

**One visible artifact.** Something in the physical world — a calendar block, a note, a
whiteboard. Not because it's a good tracker but because it produces the trigger to look.

**Name the escape hatch in advance.** *"If you miss two weeks in a row, that's not a
failure, it's a signal to halve the goal. We do that rather than stopping."* Deciding this
before the miss is the entire trick; after the miss, nobody's in a state to make that call
sensibly.

## You do the tracking

This is a real advantage the skill has over a notebook, and it should be used hard.

Every time they open a session, **read the files first and tell them where they are before
they ask**:

> Last time — 12 May — you set two things. Ravi, four study sessions a week; you've logged
> three weeks, averaging two and a half. Priya, three runs a week, and the last entry was
> 2 June. Ravi, you also agreed Sunday would be off, and it's come up twice since.

That paragraph is the product. It takes them zero effort, it's accurate, it removes the
need for anyone to remember anything, and it does the one thing self-tracking never does:
it notices what they haven't noticed.

Rules for doing it well:

- **State it flatly.** No praise, no concern, no tone. The moment tracking sounds like
  judgement, the numbers reported to you start being wrong.
- **Notice the pattern, not the week.** One bad week is noise. Three weeks in a declining
  line is worth naming. Six weeks of nothing logged means the goal is dead and should be
  said out loud rather than carried.
- **Notice absence.** A goal with no entries for a month is the most informative thing in
  the file, and it's the thing a self-managed system never surfaces because nobody goes
  looking for the thing they've been avoiding.
- **Keep the log trivial.** Appending "Ravi 3/4, Priya 1/3" to `weekly.md` is enough. If
  logging takes more than a line, it stops.

## The lapse conversation

When they've missed and are in front of you, the instinct is either to reassure or to push.
Both are wrong. Do this instead:

1. **State the fact, without adjective.** *"Two of the last four weeks."*
2. **Ask what happened, once.** Take the answer at face value. If they offer a reason,
   don't test it.
3. **Ask the useful question:** *"Is this a bad month, or is this goal not actually the
   one?"* Give them a genuine exit. Most people can't drop a goal because dropping feels
   like failing, so they carry it dead for another quarter, and its corpse discredits
   everything next to it. Making it easy to drop is what keeps the rest of the list
   credible.
4. **If it stays: halve it, and change one thing.** Same goal at the same size after a
   lapse will lapse again. Something has to be different, and "trying harder" is not a
   something.

   **Don't get here early.** The restart above is more concrete than step 3 and it's
   tempting to jump to it — halving the number feels like progress and it's a pleasant
   thing to offer. But halving a goal he was about to drop keeps a dead goal alive at a
   smaller size, which is the worse outcome. Step 3 has to be answered first, out loud,
   before any renegotiation of the number.
5. **If it goes: delete it visibly.** Move it to a parked list, say it out loud. A clean
   drop is far better for the system than a quiet carry-forward.

## Motivation, and reading it correctly

Before designing accountability, know what's actually driving it. The design differs.

Ask, and listen for which of these it is:

- **Something happened.** A health scare, a parent's illness, a birthday with a zero, a
  redundancy. Urgent and real, but the energy fades as the event recedes. *Design for the
  fade: get one thing established as a routine within six weeks, while the energy lasts.*
- **A slow accumulation.** A few years went by, nothing's wrong, nothing's moving. Low
  urgency, high persistence. *This one can take a longer arc, but it needs a reason to
  start now, because a background feeling never produces a start date.*
- **One of them wants it.** The other is participating. *Say it out loud, kindly. Then
  design something the reluctant one gets something from, or shrink it to the willing
  one's own goals with the other as support only.*
- **External pressure.** A parent, a peer group, a comparison. *Weakest fuel there is. Ask
  what they'd want if nobody were watching, and build from whatever survives that
  question.*

The distinction that matters most: **is this goal theirs, or is it one they think they
should have?** Test: *"When did you last choose this over something easier?"* No example in
the last year means it's a wish, and wishes need a different conversation — usually the
values one — rather than a better tracking system. No accountability design rescues a goal
nobody actually wants; it just produces a more precise record of not doing it.

## What good looks like at three months

Calibrate them, because their expectations are probably wrong and disappointment is what
kills this.

- **One goal each, mostly done.** Not six goals fully done.
- **Roughly 80% on lead measures.** Missed weeks are normal and expected. A quarter with
  no missed weeks usually means the goal was too small.
- **The check-in has happened most weeks.** Not every week. Missing one and resuming is
  the skill; missing one and stopping is the failure.
- **At least one goal dropped on purpose.** A couple who has never dropped a goal is
  either not being honest about what they want or not setting goals that cost anything.
- **They can say what they're working on without looking it up.** This is the real test. If
  neither can name it from memory, it isn't live, regardless of what the file says.

Say these numbers out loud at the start, not at the review. Expectations set in advance
are the difference between "we're at 80%, this is working" and "we missed three weeks,
this isn't working" — from identical data.
