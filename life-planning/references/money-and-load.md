# Money and household load

Two sessions, 45–60 minutes each. Separate topics, filed together because they share a
property: both are joint by construction, both are usually carried unevenly, and both
produce arguments that appear to be about something small.

Run these when a values or goals session keeps hitting money, or when the load question in
the weekly check-in keeps producing the same answer from the same person.

# Money

The premise: money arguments are rarely about money. They're about two different sets of
beliefs, formed in childhood, that neither person has ever articulated — what the
literature calls money scripts. Two people can look at the same balance and one sees
safety while the other sees a wasted decade, and both are responding accurately to their
own history.

So do the history before the numbers. A budget conversation held before this one is a
negotiation between two positions nobody understands.

## 1. Where it comes from (20 min)

Independently written, then read out. Predict the other's answers first.

- **What's your first memory involving money?** Not the earliest fact — the first memory
  with a feeling attached.
- What did your parents teach you about money, deliberately? What did you learn from
  watching them, that they didn't intend?
- Was money talked about in your house, or not? What happened when it was?
- Did you ever feel your family didn't have enough? What did that feel like from inside?
- What does having money mean to you — what does it get you that isn't purchasing power?

The reveal is the session. Most couples have never heard each other's answer to the first
question, and it usually explains a decade of friction in about four minutes.

## 2. What money is for (10 min)

Independently, then compare:

> Complete: "Money is for ______."

Common and very different answers: freedom, safety, options, proof I got somewhere, taking
care of people, not having to think about it. Neither is wrong. They lead to completely
different behaviour under the same balance sheet.

Then:
- What's the number at which you'd feel safe? Say it out loud. (Wildly different answers
  here are normal and worth knowing about.)
- What would you spend on without hesitating that they'd question?
- What do they spend on that you find hard, and what's the feeling underneath — is it the
  money, or something the spending represents?

## 3. Ratings, not arguments (10 min)

Take the categories they actually spend on — housing, travel, education, family support,
eating out, gifts, savings, giving, hobbies, the kids' things. Each rates every category
independently, 1–5:

**1 = I'd cut this tomorrow · 5 = I'd protect this over almost anything**

Then compare, and only discuss the categories with a gap of two or more. This is a
deliberately mechanical device and it works: it turns a values conversation into a short
list of specific disagreements, and it stops them relitigating the whole of their
financial life every time one of them buys something.

For each gap, one question each: *"What does that category protect for you?"*

## 4. Decide the structure (15 min)

Practical, and much easier now:

- What's jointly decided, and what's each person's own to spend without discussion? Name
  an actual number for the second one. The absence of an agreed no-questions amount is
  behind an enormous share of couples' money friction — it turns every personal purchase
  into a joint decision, which is exhausting for both of them.
- What's the threshold above which we talk first?
- Who does what — bills, investments, tax, tracking? Note that whoever does it holds the
  mental load of it, which is real work. See the load section below.
- When do we look at this together, and how often? Put it in the calendar. Monthly is
  usually right; if either finds money conversations stressful, shorter and more frequent
  beats long and rare.
- What's the one number we both watch?

Write the agreements and the ratings to the Money section of the file.

# Household load

Based on Eve Rodsky's Fair Play. The problem it addresses isn't chores — most couples can
divide tasks. It's the **mental load**: the noticing, remembering, planning and
anticipating, which is invisible, unbounded, and almost never counted.

Rodsky's research found the two things partners reported hating most were, on one side,
being nagged, and on the other, carrying the mental load. Those are the same problem from
two ends. Nagging is what carrying the whole load sounds like from the outside.

Say this at the start: **the goal is not a 50/50 split.** It's an arrangement both believe
is fair, where each item has one owner who holds all of it. Chasing an exact split is what
makes this conversation go badly.

## 1. Make it visible (20 min)

Build the full list together, on screen. Not "cooking" — the actual units. Meal planning.
Groceries. Cooking. Clearing up. Noticing when things run out.

Cover, at minimum: food, laundry, cleaning, bills, home maintenance, the car, medical
appointments (each person's, and the children's), school admin, extracurriculars, holidays
and travel, gifts and cards, extended family contact, social calendar, insurance and
paperwork, pets, tax, anything with a renewal date.

Then the layer people forget, and add these explicitly as their own items:

- Remembering birthdays
- Knowing what's in the fridge
- Knowing which clothes still fit the kids
- Noticing when someone's low and doing something about it
- Being the one both families call
- Keeping the calendar in your head
- Being the person the school contacts

Expect the list to be much longer than either anticipated. That's the point of the
exercise, and it's worth pausing on when it happens.

## 2. Who holds each one (15 min)

Go through the list. For each item, who currently holds it? Not who does the task — who
**remembers that it needs doing**. Those are different, and the difference is the whole
subject.

Do this before renegotiating anything. The tally is often surprising to one of them and
not at all surprising to the other, and that asymmetry is itself worth naming out loud.

The predict move applies and is sharp here: before going through the list, ask each to
guess what share they currently hold. Then count. The gap between estimate and count is
frequently the most useful number produced in this session.

## 3. Full ownership, not delegation (15 min)

The Fair Play rule that does the work: **whoever owns an item owns all three parts of it**
— noticing it needs doing, planning it, and doing it.

"Just tell me what to do and I'll do it" sounds cooperative and isn't a solution. It keeps
the noticing and planning with one person, which is the part that's actually heavy. If one
person still holds the whole list in their head and hands out tasks, nothing has moved.

So, for each item being handed over:

- **Full transfer.** The new owner holds all three parts.
- **Agreed standard.** What "done" means, agreed once, in advance. Most handovers fail
  here — one person's acceptable and the other's acceptable are different, and the
  disagreement gets read as not caring. Settle it up front and then let it go.
- **No taking it back.** The person handing over has to actually stop noticing it, which
  is harder than it sounds and is the real cost of the transfer. Say this explicitly and
  get agreement. If they can't let go of an item, it might be one to keep.
- **Some things stay unbalanced, on purpose.** If one person's job is genuinely more
  demanding this year, an uneven split can be right — as long as it's named, agreed, and
  has a review date, rather than being a default nobody chose.

## 4. Write it down and set a review (10 min)

To the Household Load table in the file: item, owner, standard, review date.

Review at the quarterly. Loads drift back — quietly, without a decision, usually toward
whoever notices things faster — and the table is what makes the drift visible instead of
resentful.

## Traps

**Debating rather than listing.** They start negotiating on item four and never finish the
list. Hold the line: list everything first, negotiate nothing until it's all visible. The
list *is* the intervention.

**Scoring.** It turns into a tally of who does more. Redirect: the question is whether the
current arrangement is one you'd both choose, not who's winning.

**The invisible items get skipped.** They feel too small to write down, and they're the
whole reason for the session. Insist on them.

**Agreeing to a transfer and then hovering.** Predictable, and it's not bad faith — it's a
habit built over years. Name it in advance as the likely failure mode, and put it on the
weekly check-in for a few weeks.
