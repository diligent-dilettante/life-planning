# Values session

The aim is not a list of nice words. It's a short set of statements they recognise as
true, specific enough that you could tell from watching a week whether it's being lived.
"Family" is not a value; "I want to be the parent who's at pickup, not the one paying for
pickup" is.

**Default to the short version.** The full one is good and most people don't need it on a
first pass. Offer it; don't assume it.

---

## The twenty-minute version

Four questions. Do this unless they've specifically asked for something deeper.

**1. A day you'd live again.**
> Think of a day in the last couple of years you'd live again exactly as it was. Not a
> holiday — an ordinary day works better. What actually happened?

Then the question that does the work:
> What was in that day that isn't in most days?

**2. The disproportionate irritation.**
> What's something small that irritates you far more than it should? Something where you
> know the reaction is out of proportion.

Disproportionate irritation is nearly always a violated value, and it surfaces the ones
people don't list when asked directly, because they're not flattering. Ask what it's
protecting.

**3. Name three, with a cost.**
From those two answers, name three things that matter. For each, one line on what it means
in their own words, and one line on **what it costs them.** The cost line is what makes it
real — a value with no cost is a preference. If they can't name a cost, it probably isn't
one of their three.

**4. The gap.**
> Nought to ten, how closely have the last three months matched each of these?

Take the one with the biggest gap that they actually care about, and ask: *what's one
change in the next month that moves it a single point?* Not to ten. One point.

That's the session. Write the three values and the one change. Then:

**If there's a partner** — one question worth asking them, offered because the answer is
interesting rather than as an input to anything: *"What's a day in the last couple of years
you'd live again exactly as it was?"* Say it's better asked without explaining why.

Ask what they think the answer will be before they hear it. Skip all of this if there's no
partner, or if it would land as homework.

---

## The full version

60–90 minutes, and worth doing eventually — vision and goals both reference back to it.
Steps 1–4 are theirs alone; her half happens separately.

## Shape of the session

Steps 1–4 are done **independently and in silence**. No talking, no peeking. This is
non-negotiable in a values session — values are exactly the thing people unconsciously
adjust to match their partner's, and once adjusted they can't be unadjusted.

Say this up front so it doesn't feel odd: *"The first half of this is you both writing on
your own without talking. That's deliberate — if you hear each other's answers first, you
get one person's values twice."*

## 1. Peak moments (15 min)

Values are easier to reverse-engineer from evidence than to introspect directly.

Ask each of them, writing alone:

> Think of two or three days in the last couple of years you'd live again exactly as
> they were. Not holidays or big occasions necessarily — ordinary days work better.
> Write down what actually happened, in detail. Who was there, what you were doing,
> what time you woke up, what the room looked like.

Give five minutes. Then, still alone:

> Look at what you wrote. What was present in those days that isn't in most days?
> What were you getting that you don't usually get?

That second question is where the values come from. Don't skip it, and don't let them
answer it out loud yet.

**If someone is stuck on peak moments**, flip it: a day in the last year they'd pay money
never to repeat, and what was missing from it.

## 2. What reliably annoys you (10 min)

Still silent, still separate.

> What's something small that irritates you far more than it should? Something where
> you know your reaction is disproportionate.

Disproportionate irritation is almost always a violated value. Someone who can't stand
being interrupted mid-sentence values being taken seriously. Someone who seethes at
waste values something about stewardship or effort. This exercise produces the values
people don't list when asked directly, because they're not flattering.

Have each of them write the irritation and then a guess at what it's protecting.

## 3. Narrow the list (15 min)

Show them the [values list](#values-list) below. Independently:

- Circle everything that lands. Don't think hard — go fast, aim for 15–25.
- Cut to 10. This is uncomfortable and it should be.
- Cut to 5. Rank them 1–5.

The cutting is the exercise. If someone can't get below eight, ask: *"If you could only
live one of these fully for the next five years, and the others only partly, which one?"*

Anything from steps 1 and 2 that isn't on the list gets added in their own words. Their
own phrasing usually beats the list word.

## 4. Define them properly (15 min)

For each of their five, still writing alone, three lines:

1. **What it means to me** — one sentence, their own words, not a dictionary definition.
2. **What I'd be doing** — what would an observer see in a week where this was fully
   alive? Needs a verb and a frequency.
3. **What it costs** — what do I give up to live this? A value with no cost isn't a
   value, it's a preference. This line is the one that makes the whole exercise real.

Example, done properly:

> **Craft.** I'd rather do one thing to a standard I'm proud of than three things
> adequately.
> *Doing:* saying no to at least one thing a month that I could do but wouldn't do well.
> *Costs:* money, and being seen as less ambitious than I am.

Example, done badly:

> **Excellence.** I believe in always doing my best.

If you get the second kind, ask for the cost line first. It forces specificity.

## 5. Predict, then reveal (15 min)

Now they talk. Order matters here.

**First, prediction.** Before either reads theirs out, ask each of them:

> What do you think their top five are going to be?

Write both predictions down. This is often the single most interesting moment of the
session — sometimes because one of them nails it, more often because of what they miss
or what they assume. Don't rush past a miss. *"You thought she'd say security. She said
adventure. What made you expect security?"*

**Then reveal.** One reads all five with their definitions, uninterrupted. The other
listens, then says back what they heard before responding. Swap. No cross-talk in
between.

**Then curiosity, one round each:**

- Which of theirs surprised you?
- Which of theirs have you been getting in the way of, without meaning to?
- Which of theirs do you find hardest to live with?

That last question is uncomfortable and worth asking. It's better named in a calm session
than discovered in an argument.

## 6. Find the shared set (15 min)

Sort the ten values into three groups on screen:

- **Shared** — both named it, or both immediately agree it belongs to the household
- **Adjacent** — different words, same underlying thing. Test it: ask each what they'd be
  doing. If the behaviour is the same, it's adjacent. If not, it's actually divergent
- **Individually held** — one person's, and that's completely fine

Say the "that's completely fine" part out loud. Couples often arrive expecting values to
match and read difference as a bad sign. It isn't. Two people with identical values have
no one to check them.

Then pick **three to five shared values**, no more, and for each write:

> **[Value].** We'd know we were living it if: [something observable, within a month].

Push hard on that second line. "We'd know we were living generosity if we were more
generous" is not an answer. "If we'd had someone over for dinner twice a month and paid
for something without being asked" is.

**Where they diverge and it matters**, don't resolve it now. Write it in the file under
open items with what each position protects. Divergent values are a Vision session
problem, not a Values session problem.

## 7. The bull's eye (10 min)

Adapted from Tobias Lundgren's ACT Bull's Eye. This converts values into a gap you can
act on.

For each of their own five values, each person rates, privately then shared:

> Over the last three months, how closely have your actual weeks matched this value?
> Zero is not at all, ten is fully.

Then, together:

- Which value has the biggest gap between how much it matters and how you're living it?
- Not "which is worst" — which gap do you most want to close?
- What's one change in the next month that moves it a single point? Not to ten. One point.

That one-point change is the bridge into the Goals session. Write it down even if you're
not doing goals today.

## Output

Write to each person's file:

```markdown
## Values — as of 2026-08-15

### 1. Craft
What it means: I'd rather do one thing well than three adequately.
What I'd be doing: saying no to at least one thing a month.
What it costs: money, and looking less ambitious than I am.
Living it (0-10): 4
```

Write to the shared sections of the file:

```markdown
## Shared values — as of 2026-08-15

**Craft** — we'd know we were living it if neither of us took on work in a quarter
that we knew we'd do badly.

## Individually held
Ravi: adventure, independence
Priya: security, closeness to family

## Open
Ravi values adventure; Priya values proximity to her parents. Both real, currently
in tension over the question of moving. Ravi's protects: not feeling settled too
early. Priya's protects: her mother's health. Revisit in the vision session.
```

Also write the full raw session notes, including the predictions and the peak-moment
stories, into Notes Worth Keeping. The stories are worth keeping — they're what makes the values
mean something when read back in a year.

## Traps

**Aspirational values.** People list who they'd like to be. Test: *"When did you last
choose this over something easier?"* If there's no example in the last year, it's a wish.
Wishes go in the Vision session, not here. Say that without judgement — it's a useful
finding, not a failure.

**Inherited values.** Values absorbed from parents, community, or profession that were
never examined. Test: *"Who would be disappointed if you dropped this?"* If the answer is
a person rather than themselves, dig one level.

**The peace-keeping merge.** One partner adjusting mid-session to match the other. You'll
see it as a value appearing in the second reveal that wasn't in the first prediction, or
a definition that shifts after hearing the partner's. If you spot it, don't accuse — ask
neutrally: *"Was that on your list before we started?"*

**Vagueness as protection.** See `facilitation.md`. Someone who only offers abstractions
usually has something specific they're not ready to say. Narrow the aperture, don't push.
