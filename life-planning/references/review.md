# Review sessions

---

## The thirty-minute quarterly

Use this by default. The full versions are below and are worth it once a year at most.

**1. Read the goals back exactly as they were written.** People misremember what they
committed to, reliably in a softening direction. Then give them the numbers from the log
before they guess.

**2. The four-box question.** For the goal, two separate things:

- Did you do the thing you said you'd do each week?
- Did the goal happen?

|  | Goal happened | Didn't |
|---|---|---|
| **Did the weekly thing** | The plan works. Reuse it. | The plan was wrong, or the goal was. You didn't fail — your model did. |
| **Didn't** | Luck, or it was easier than you thought. Don't draw lessons. | The ordinary case. Go to the obstacle. |

Most people collapse all four into "did I hit it", and so learn nothing from the most
informative quarter they'll get — the one where they did everything right and it still
didn't work. Say which box they're in, out loud.

**3. Was the obstacle the one you predicted?** Usually it isn't, and the real one is the
finding. Did the if–then ever actually fire? If it never fired, either the obstacle was
wrong or the plan was too vague.

**4. Keep, drop, or change.** Give explicit permission to drop: *"Do you still want this,
or do you just not want to be the person who dropped it?"* Anything carried forward
doesn't carry unchanged — what's different this time?

**5. Update the history table.** Then look at it and say what you see. Three quarters in,
patterns start showing up that they can't see from inside: which kinds of goals get done,
when things stall, what the stalls have in common. This is the payoff for keeping the
file, and it's the thing they can't do for themselves.

**6. Book the next one.**

**If there's someone who watched the quarter** — partner, close friend, colleague:
*"What did you see me do this quarter that I'm probably not giving myself credit for? And
what did you see get in the way that I might not have noticed?"* A pattern that kills a
goal is far more visible from the next chair than from inside it. If there's nobody to ask,
the history table does this job instead, which is what it's for.

## The full versions

Two different sessions live here. Check which one they want before reading further.

- **Quarterly review** — 60 minutes, at the end of a twelve-week goal cycle. Score, learn,
  reset. Usually flows straight into a new Goals session on another day.
- **Annual review** — two sittings of about 90 minutes. Look back honestly at a year,
  then decide what the next one is for.

# Quarterly review

Read Live Right Now and the check-in log first, and open by reading the goals back exactly
as they were written twelve weeks ago. People misremember what they committed to, almost
always in a softening direction.

## 1. Score it (15 min)

For each goal, in this order:

- **Lead measures:** what percentage of planned actions actually happened? A real number.
- **The goal itself:** done, partly done, or not done?
- **Was the plan followed?** Separate from whether the goal was achieved. This distinction
  is the whole point of the exercise, because the four combinations mean different things:

| | Goal achieved | Not achieved |
|---|---|---|
| **Plan followed** | The plan works. Reuse it. | The plan was wrong, or the goal was. You didn't fail — your model did. |
| **Plan not followed** | Luck, or the goal was easier than you thought. Don't draw lessons from it. | The ordinary case. Go to the obstacle. |

Most people collapse all four into "did I hit it", and so learn nothing from a quarter
where they did everything right and it didn't work. That quarter is the most informative
one they'll get.

## 2. What actually happened (15 min)

Predict first: *"What do you think their honest score is?"* Then ask each other, since
each has watched the other's quarter from outside:

- **What did you see them do that they're not giving themselves credit for?**
- **What did you see get in the way that they might not have noticed?** Asked kindly, and
  it's genuinely useful — the pattern that killed a goal is usually more visible from
  the next chair than from inside it.

Then, for each goal that didn't work:

- Was the obstacle the one you predicted in the WOOP? Usually it isn't, and the real one
  is the interesting finding.
- Did the if-then plan ever get used? If it never fired, either the obstacle was wrong or
  the plan was too vague.
- Did the support ask happen? Ask both — the answers frequently differ, and that gap is
  worth ten minutes on its own.

## 3. Keep, kill, change (15 min)

For each goal: carry it forward, drop it, or change it.

Give explicit permission to drop. A goal carried into a third quarter out of guilt costs
more than it's worth — it sits on the list, gets scored badly every week, and quietly
teaches both of them that the list is aspirational rather than real. Ask plainly: *"Do you
still want this, or do you just not want to be the person who dropped it?"*

For anything carried forward: it doesn't carry unchanged. What's different this time?

## 4. Back to values (15 min)

Reopen the bull's-eye ratings from the last Values session. Each re-rates, 0–10, how
closely the last three months matched each of their values.

- Which moved? Which didn't?
- Was the movement caused by anything you did on purpose?
- Anything on the values list you now think isn't actually yours?

That last question matters. Values shift, and a values list nobody has permission to
revise turns into a monument. Update the Values section with the new ratings and the date;
keep the old ones visible so the trend is legible.

Then close: book the next Goals session for a different day, and book the next quarterly
review twelve weeks out.

# Annual review — part one, looking back

Do this before deciding anything about next year. Deciding first and reviewing after is
just resolutions with extra steps.

## Homework: the calendar sweep

This part is done **alone**, before the session, and it's the part that carries the whole
thing. From Tim Ferriss's Past Year Review.

> Open your calendar and go week by week through the whole year. Two columns: positive and
> negative. Every person, place, activity and commitment that produced a genuine peak —
> good or bad — goes in a column. Don't summarise. Individual entries.

An hour, roughly. Then:

> Look at the positive column. **Which 20% of these produced most of the good?** Circle
> them. Now the negative column: which 20% produced most of the misery?

Then the two commitments that make it useful:

> Schedule more of the positive 20% into next year's calendar now, today.
> And write down the negative 20% as a list of things to say no to. Put it somewhere
> you'll see it when the phone rings.

The reason this beats reflecting from memory: memory keeps the story, and the story is
edited. The calendar keeps the evidence. People are routinely wrong about what actually
made their year good, and the calendar is what corrects them.

## In the session

**Predict, then reveal.** Before either reads their columns:

> What do you think was the best thing in their year? What do you think was the worst?

Then read the 20% lists to each other. Then, one at a time, out loud:

**On the year that happened**
- What are you most proud of, that nobody else would know to congratulate you for?
- What was harder than you expected?
- What did you give up this year? Was it on purpose?
- Who mattered most this year? Do they know?
- What's the thing you kept meaning to deal with and didn't?

**On each other** — the questions that need two people
- What did you watch them go through this year that you don't think they've fully
  registered?
- What did they carry for you this year?
- What was the best month for us? What was the worst? *(Ask separately first. Different
  answers here are common and informative.)*
- What did we get wrong as a pair this year?

**On the process itself**
- Did last year's goals survive? If they didn't, what's the honest reason?
- What did we plan that we no longer want?

Take the break here. Part two is better on a different day — the looking-back session
produces a mood, and decisions made in that mood are either too harsh or too sentimental.

# Annual review — part two, looking forward

## 1. What's the year for (20 min)

Independently, then reveal:

> If next year had one job, what would it be? One sentence.

Then compare. A shared answer is convenient; a different answer is more useful, because
now you know. Some years are legitimately one person's year — a career move, a
qualification, a recovery — and saying so explicitly is far better than both people
half-pursuing everything. Ask directly: *"Is this one of those years where it's mostly
about one of us? Which one?"*

That question is uncomfortable and it's the most valuable one in the session.

## 2. What to stop (10 min)

Usually skipped, and it's where the capacity comes from:

- What are we stopping this year?
- What are we saying no to when it's offered? (The negative 20% list is already this
  answer — read it back.)
- What are we spending money on that neither of us actually wants?

## 3. Check the vision still holds (15 min)

Read the anchors from the file aloud.

- Is each of these still true?
- Anything that's quietly stopped being true and we've been pretending?
- Anything new that should be an anchor?

Anchors should change slowly. If they change every year they weren't anchors. If they've
never changed in five years, ask whether anyone's tested them.

## 4. Then stop (5 min)

Do **not** set the year's goals here. Book a Goals session for a separate day and set the
first twelve weeks then.

Annual goals set at the end of an annual review are set on a wave of clarity that doesn't
survive February. The twelve-week cycle is what makes them real, and it wants its own
session with a clear head.

Write the year's job, the stop list, and the reviewed anchors to `reviews/2026-annual.md`
to the file under Notes Worth Keeping, and update the Anchors. Book the Goals session before ending.

## Traps

**Reviewing from memory.** Without the calendar sweep, an annual review is a mood.
Insist on the homework, even a rough version.

**The good-news review.** Only positives. Ask directly what they're not saying. If it's
still absent, ask each of them the same question about the *other* person's year — it's
often easier to be honest about, and it opens the door.

**Scoring as verdict.** A quarter where the plan was followed and the goal missed is a
successful experiment, not a failure. Say so, in those words. Couples who treat scoring
as a verdict stop scoring honestly by the second quarter, and then the whole apparatus is
theatre.

**Rolling everything forward.** Every unfinished goal carried into the next cycle. The
list grows, execution drops, faith in the list disappears. Force a drop.

**Reviewing and planning in one sitting.** They're different moods and they contaminate
each other. Two sittings, different days.
