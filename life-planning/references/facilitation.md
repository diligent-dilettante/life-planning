## Opening

**There is only one intake, and it's the one in `SKILL.md`.** Don't run that and then run
a second list of opening questions from here — between them that's eight questions before
anything has happened, and eight questions is an intake form, not a conversation.

The `SKILL.md` intake (why now / tried before / is she in / what would make this worth an
hour) is for a **first engagement**. Don't repeat it in later sessions.

On a returning session the opening is three lines, not four questions:

1. **Read the file and say where they are.** Before they ask.
2. **Ask what they want to do today**, and take the answer.
3. **Ask how long they've got**, and hold it. A twenty-minute session done well beats an
   hour that overruns and never gets repeated.

Anything else you'd want to know — which shape the session is, whether they're carrying
something in — ask only when it's actually load-bearing for what you're about to do. In a
ten-minute check-in it never is.

## Answer before you see hers

The core mechanic, and the asynchronous setup makes it easy.

Whenever something generative is on the table — values, what a good year looks like, what
they want in five years — **their answer gets written before they see hers.** Not because
her answer is contaminating in itself, but because whoever answers second unconsciously
edits toward the first. They don't notice it happening. Neither does she. The couple then
concludes they agree, when what actually happened is that one of them never spoke.

In practice:

- They answer here, fully, and it goes in the file.
- She answers separately — her own conversation, or on paper. Give them the question to
  take to her, worded so it can just be asked. Not "discuss values with your wife."
- **Before showing them her answer, ask what they think it is.** Write the prediction down
  next to the real one.

That prediction step is worth more than most of the exercises. Sometimes they're exactly
right, which tells them something. More often they're wrong in a specific direction, and
the direction of the error is the finding. Don't rush past a miss: *"You expected her to
say security. She said adventure. What made you expect security?"*

## Bringing her answers back

When they've got her answers in hand:

1. **Predict first**, if it hasn't happened already. Always.
2. **Record hers verbatim.** Her words, not their paraphrase of her words. If they're
   paraphrasing, ask for the actual phrasing — *"is that how she put it?"*
3. **Read both, then discuss.** Not one at a time with commentary in between.
4. **Mark what she hasn't seen.** Anything in the file that's their account of a shared
   matter stays flagged until she's actually looked at it. A file that quietly turns one
   person's view into "our vision" does real damage.

If they only have a rough version of what she said, record it as their impression of her
answer and say so in the file. That's honest and still useful. Recording it as hers is
not.

## Working with difference

When their answers diverge, the instinct is to close the gap fast. Resist it for a few
minutes — the compromise reached in the first minute is a split-the-difference deal
neither of them wants.

Go underneath:

- What does your version make possible that hers doesn't?
- What are you protecting?
- What would have to be true for you to be fine with her version?
- Is there a version where you both get the thing underneath?

Surface positions are often incompatible while the underlying needs aren't. One wants to
move and one doesn't; underneath, one wants a bigger professional stage and one wants her
parents twenty minutes away. Those two have more solutions than move/don't-move allows.

If it stays genuinely incompatible, don't manufacture agreement. It goes in **Open** in the
file, with what each position protects. Some resolve on their own in six months. A forced
resolution recorded as consensus is worse than an honest open item, because it forecloses
the conversation while leaving the disagreement fully intact.

## When someone is vague

Vagueness is usually protective, not lazy. "I just want us to be happy" means either they
haven't looked, or they have and aren't ready to say it. Don't interrogate. Narrow the
aperture:

- **Recent past:** "Think of the last month — when did that feel true?"
- **Absence:** "When did you notice it missing?" People are far more precise about what's
  missing than about what they want.
- **Small:** "What's one hour of a week that would tell you it was working?"
- **A menu, not an answer:** "Some people mean X, some mean Y — any of those close?"

After two attempts, leave it. Write "not yet clear" and come back. Manufactured clarity is
worse than an honest blank, and it tends to get built on.

## Questions worth having on hand

**One question per turn, after the intake.** The intake in `SKILL.md` is the single
exception — two or three questions in that first turn is fine, because they're orienting
questions and the person expects them. Everywhere else, and in every turn after the first,
ask one thing and stop. Two questions in a turn means they answer the second and the first
is quietly lost, and the first is usually the one you wanted.


- What would I actually see, if I were watching?
- Give me the most recent example.
- What did you do about it?

**To find what's underneath**
- What's good about that? *(repeat two or three times — it ladders up to a value)*
- If you got that, what would it get you?
- What would you lose if you gave it up?

**When something feels immovable**
- *Exception:* when was it last a bit better? What was different?
- *Scaling:* nought to ten, where is it? Why that and not two lower? What's a half point
  higher look like, concretely, next week?
- *Miracle:* suppose you woke up and it was sorted, but nobody told you. What's the first
  thing you'd notice? What would she notice?

**To test whether they mean it**
- What are you willing to give up for this?
- What have you already tried?
- Out of ten, how likely are you to actually do it? If under eight — what would make it
  an eight?

**About her, when she isn't here**
- What do you think she'd say?
- What does she do that helps with this that she probably doesn't know helps?
- What's one specific thing you need from her?
- What would her version of that story be?

## Writing it down

- **Their words.** If she said "I want to stop being the one who does all the
  remembering", write that. Not "improve household task distribution". The original
  sentence is the one she'll recognise in November.
- **Read back before writing.** Always.
- **Attribute.** Who said it, and when.
- **The history table is never deleted from.** Goals that got dropped stay, with what got
  in the way. That table is where the patterns come from, and the patterns are the most
  valuable thing in the file.
- **Ask what stays out.**

## Failure modes

| What happens | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Her voice disappears | The file is entirely his account; her sections are guesses | Flag unseen items; give them one specific thing to ask her each session |
| Premature merge | Two different answers become one bland shared one | Record both; sit in the difference for five minutes |
| The nice document | Everything agreed, nothing specific | "What would I see you doing?" until there's a verb and a day |
| Session sprawl | Values turns into vision turns into money turns into an argument | One session, one job. Park the rest by name |
| Facilitator takeover | You're suggesting values and goals | Count questions against statements. If statements are winning, stop |
| The wish list | Eleven goals | Cap at three. Making them choose *is* the session |
| Evaporation | Good session, nothing changes | One action, one owner, one date. Every time |
| Quiet drift | Sessions still happen; nothing in Live Right Now has moved in two months | Say it plainly. Then `keeping-it-going.md` |
