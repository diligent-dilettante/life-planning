# Vision session

Values told them what matters. Vision asks what they'd build with it. The output is not a
mission statement — it's a few concrete anchors specific enough to rule things out. A
vision that rules nothing out isn't doing any work.

---

## The thirty-minute version

**1. An ordinary Tuesday, three years out.**

Five years is abstract enough to stay pleasant. Three years and a normal weekday is where
it gets concrete.

> It's three years from now and things have gone well. Not spectacularly — just well.
> It's an ordinary Tuesday. Where do you wake up? What's the first hour? Who else is in
> the house? What do you do until lunch? Who do you see? What's the evening?

Push for detail, and then ask the question that makes it real:

> What's the thing you're mildly annoyed about? There's always one.

A fantasy Tuesday has no irritations. The version with a small complaint in it is the one
they actually believe.

**2. The one they'd never say out loud.**
> If money and what people think were genuinely not factors — what would you be doing
> in five years?

If the answer looks a lot like their current life, ask which it is: *"Is that because
you'd choose this again, or because it's hard to let yourself want something else?"* Both
are real answers. The second one is worth more.

**3. Two or three anchors.**
From the Tuesday, what has to be true? Push until each one rules something out.

> "We want to be financially secure" — rules out nothing.
> "Neither of us takes a job with more than four nights away a month" — rules out plenty.

That's the session. Write the Tuesday and the anchors.

**If there's a partner** — the Tuesday question, asked exactly as written. Ask what they
think the answer will be first, particularly where the other person wakes up. Skip it if
there's nobody to ask; the Tuesday works perfectly well as their own.

## The full version

90 minutes, and it works far better if the writing happens beforehand.

## Homework, sent in advance

Ask them each to write two short pieces separately, a few days before. Adapted from
Burnett and Evans's Designing Your Life. About 250 words each, written alone, not shown to
each other until the session.

**Lifeview** — what's it all for?
> Why are we here? What makes a life good, as opposed to comfortable? What do you owe
> other people? What role do money, family, and work play in it? What do you think happens
> when it's over, if anything, and does that change how you'd spend a Tuesday?

**Workview** — what's work for?
> What is work actually for, beyond money? What makes work good work? What's the
> relationship between work and the rest of a life? How much is enough?

Most people have never written either down. The value is that the two pieces have to
cohere — if the lifeview says presence and the workview says a decade of building
something at all costs, that contradiction is now on paper where it can be discussed.

**If they didn't do the homework**, don't cancel. Give them fifteen minutes at the top of
the session to write short versions, on separate sofas, phones face down.

## 1. Read the lifeviews to each other (15 min)

Predict first, as always:

> Before either of you reads: what do you think theirs says? What's the one line you
> expect?

Then one reads their lifeview aloud, uninterrupted. The other says back what they heard.
Swap. Then workviews the same way.

The questions after:

- Where do your two pieces disagree with each other?
- Where does your own workview contradict your own lifeview?

That second question is the one people flinch at. Sit with it. Most people are carrying a
work story and a life story that cannot both be true, and have never had to say them out
loud within five minutes of each other.

## 2. Three lives each (25 min)

The Odyssey Plans, from Designing Your Life. Each person sketches three different
five-year lives — **separately, on paper, no discussion**. Five to seven minutes each,
which is deliberately not enough time to be careful.

- **Life 1 — the road you're on.** Current trajectory, extended five years, assuming it
  broadly works. Not a bad life. The one that happens if nothing changes.
- **Life 2 — that road is gone.** Your industry disappears, the job's not available, the
  plan is off the table. It's not a tragedy, it just isn't an option. What now?
- **Life 3 — money and other people's opinions are irrelevant.** Nobody's judging, income
  is not the constraint. What do you do?

Each life gets: a headline of six words or fewer, a rough timeline of what happens in each
of the five years, one thing you'd have to learn, and one question the plan doesn't answer.

Life 3 is where the real information is, and it's the one people resist. If someone
produces a Life 3 that looks a lot like Life 1, that's either a very good sign or an
indication they can't yet let themselves want anything. Ask which: *"Is that because
you'd genuinely choose this again, or because it's hard to picture wanting something
else?"*

**Then predict, then reveal.** Each guesses the other's Life 3 headline before hearing it.
This is the moment of the session. People are routinely wrong about what their partner
would do if nothing were stopping them, and finding that out matters more than the plans.

## 3. Score them (15 min)

For each of their own three lives, a private 1–5 on four dials:

- **Resources** — do I have the time, money, skill and contacts?
- **Likeability** — do I actually want it, as opposed to admiring it?
- **Confidence** — could I pull it off?
- **Coherence** — is it consistent with my values, my lifeview and my workview?

Coherence is the one to interrogate. A plan scoring high on likeability and low on
coherence is usually an escape fantasy rather than a direction, and it's worth knowing
that before anyone quits anything.

## 4. The contention pass (15 min)

This step exists only because there are two of them, and it's the reason the session is
joint rather than parallel. Put all six lives side by side and ask, plainly:

- **Which combinations are impossible?** Not undesirable — physically impossible. Same
  five years, same money, same house, same calendar. Name them out loud.
- **Which of their lives requires something from you that you haven't agreed to?** A move,
  a drop in income, carrying more at home, a period of absence. Say it as a request, not
  an accusation.
- **Which of your own lives did you quietly shrink because of them?** Ask each person
  separately and give it real silence. Something almost always got trimmed before it was
  written down, and it's usually the thing that comes back in ten years as resentment.
- **Where are you in each other's plans at all?** Some Life 3s have a spouse in them and
  some conspicuously don't. Notice it without drama, and ask.

Don't try to resolve any of it in this step. Naming it is the whole job. If it gets hot,
see `facilitation.md`.

## 5. A Tuesday, three years out (15 min)

Five years is abstract enough to stay pleasant. Three years and a normal weekday is where
it gets concrete. Solution-focused, and done **together** — this is the one part where you
want them building the same picture out loud.

> It's three years from now and things have gone well — not spectacularly, just well.
> It's an ordinary Tuesday. Walk me through it. Where do you wake up? What's the first
> hour? Who else is in the house? What do you do until lunch? Who do you see? What's the
> evening? What's the thing you're mildly annoyed about, because there's always one?

Keep pushing for detail. Ask about the annoyance specifically — a fantasy Tuesday has no
irritations, and a real one does. The version with a small complaint in it is the one
they actually believe.

Build one shared Tuesday, and note where they'd each describe it differently.

## 6. What's true in all of them (10 min)

Across six plans and the Tuesday, what recurs?

- What shows up in every version, including the ones you'd never choose?
- What's absent from all of them that you'd have expected to be there?
- What's the earliest thing that would have to be true?

The recurring elements are the vision. Not the plans — the plans are disposable, and
should be. Three to six anchors is the right number.

An anchor is specific enough to rule things out. *"We want to be financially secure"*
rules nothing out. *"Neither of us takes a job that means being away more than four nights
a month"* rules out plenty, which is what makes it useful.

## Output

To the file, under Where We're Heading:

```markdown
## Vision — as of 2026-08-15

### Where we're heading
Three or four sentences, their words. Not a slogan.

### Anchors
1. Neither of us takes work that means being away more than four nights a month.
2. We stay within two hours of Priya's parents until her mother's situation changes.
3. One of us is always the one doing school pickup. Not a nanny.
4. We don't take on debt for anything that isn't the house.

### An ordinary Tuesday, 2029
[the shared picture, in detail]

### Contentions named
Ravi's Life 3 involves a year abroad. Anchor 2 rules it out for now. Both know
this. Revisit when Priya's mother's situation changes.

### What each of us shrank
Ravi trimmed the abroad year before writing it down. Priya didn't write down
studying again, and said out loud she'd assumed it was off the table.
```

Keep all six odyssey plans in the session file verbatim. They're worth reading again in
two years, particularly the ones nobody chose.

## Traps

**The brochure.** Vision statements that could belong to any couple. Fix: ask what it
rules out. If nothing, it's decoration.

**Only Life 1.** All three plans are variations on the current path. Usually means one or
both is in a mode where wanting things feels unsafe or self-indulgent. Don't push in the
session. Try: *"whose permission would you need?"*

**Merging too early.** Six plans collapsing into one joint plan in the first ten minutes.
Keep them separate through the scoring. The contention pass is the right place for the
joint view, and it's better done on plans that were honestly individual.

**Vision as prediction.** They start hedging because they can't know what happens. Say
plainly: this isn't a forecast, it's a statement of what you'd choose. It's allowed to be
wrong.

**Skipping to goals.** Very common at the ninety-minute mark, when energy is up and
someone wants to make a list. Don't. Goals set at the end of a vision session are set on
adrenaline. Book the goals session for another day and end here.
