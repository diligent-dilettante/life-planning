# When something is jammed

45 minutes. Three different things get called "stuck" and they need different tools, so
diagnose before you pick one.

Ask: **what exactly is stuck?**

| It sounds like | It is | Go to |
|---|---|---|
| "I keep saying I'll do this and I don't" | A stuck goal — behaviour that won't move despite genuine intent | [Immunity to change](#a-stuck-goal-immunity-to-change) |
| "We can't decide about X" | A decision — two options, no movement | [The decision](#a-decision) |
| "We keep having the same argument" | A recurring disagreement — a position both are defending | [The same argument again](#the-same-argument-again) |

If it's ambiguous, ask: *"If a fairy fixed one thing overnight, what would be different in
the morning?"* The answer usually sorts it.

## A stuck goal: immunity to change

Kegan and Lahey's immunity map. Use it when someone genuinely wants a change, has tried,
and it hasn't happened. The premise is not lack of willpower — it's that they hold a
second commitment, unexamined, which the change would violate. From that second
commitment's point of view, the self-sabotage is working perfectly.

Say that framing out loud before starting. It changes what people are willing to admit.

Run it for **one person at a time**, with the partner present as witness. The partner's
role matters and is specified below — this is one of the places where a second person in
the room is genuinely load-bearing.

### Column 1: the improvement goal

> What do you want to change? Something you genuinely want, that matters to you, that
> you've tried.

Must be theirs, not something they've been told to want. Test: *"Who wants this?"*

### Column 2: what you're doing instead

> List everything you do, or don't do, that works against it. No explaining, no
> justifying, just the inventory.

Behaviours only. Not "I'm not disciplined" — that's a story. "I open my laptop at 9pm and
work instead" is a behaviour.

**Then bring the partner in:** *"What else would you add to that list?"* They will have
items. This is where the second person earns their place — a partner sees the behaviour
from outside, without the running justification that accompanies it from inside. Ask them
to describe, not diagnose: what they observe, not what they think it means.

Expect this to sting slightly. Warn the person first that it's coming and get their
agreement. If they say no, respect it and move on with the list they made.

### Column 3: the hidden competing commitment

The core move, and it takes some patience.

> Look at column two. Imagine doing the exact opposite of every item — starting tomorrow,
> completely. What's the most uncomfortable feeling that comes up?

Sit in that. Don't rush it. Then:

> So there's a part of you committed to not feeling that. What's it committed to?

Phrase it as a commitment: *I am also committed to never being seen as unreliable.*
*I am also committed to not being the kind of person who puts himself first.*
*I am also committed to not finding out what I'm capable of, because then I'd know.*

You'll know it's the real one when the person goes quiet or laughs uncomfortably. Polite
agreement means you haven't got it yet — go back to the feeling.

**The partner's question here:** *"Does that sound like them?"* They usually recognise it
faster than the person does, and often can date it. Don't let them answer it *for* the
person, though — the naming has to be first-person.

### Column 4: the big assumption

> For that commitment to make sense, what would have to be true about the world?

*If I put myself first, people will leave.* *If I try properly and fail, I'll have found
out something I can't unknow.* *If I'm not the reliable one, I'm not needed.*

Stated plainly, most big assumptions are visibly not universally true. That's the point —
they've been operating unexamined, and examination is most of the treatment.

### The test

Don't try to demolish the assumption. Design a small, safe, reversible test of it.

> What's the smallest thing you could do this month that would give you real information
> about whether that's true? Small enough that if the worst happens, it's survivable.

Not "put myself first from now on." More like: *say no to one request this month and watch
what actually happens.*

**The partner's job after the session:** they know the map now. Agree one specific thing —
usually noticing a column-two behaviour out loud, once, without commentary. Get the wording
agreed in the room. "Doing the 9pm thing again" is fine if both agree to it; anything that
could be delivered with an edge should be reworded now, not tested later.

## A decision

For genuine two-option decisions with no movement — move or stay, take the job or don't,
have another child or don't.

### 1. Surface the real criteria (15 min)

Independently, before discussing:

> What would have to be true for you to say yes without hesitating?
> What would have to be true for you to say no without regret?

Then predict each other's answers before reading them out. This routinely produces the
finding that they've been arguing about the option while holding compatible criteria — or
that they've been agreeing about the option for entirely different reasons, which is
worse, because it breaks later.

### 2. Establish the type

- **Reversible or not?** If reversible, the cost of deciding wrong is small and the cost
  of not deciding is compounding. Say so. A lot of stuck decisions are reversible ones
  being treated as permanent.
- **Is it actually two options?** Two-option framings usually mean the third one hasn't
  been looked for. Spend five minutes on: *"What's a version neither of us has proposed?"*
  Ask it even when it seems obviously binary.
- **What's the deadline?** If there isn't a real one, decide now whether to set one or to
  explicitly park it. "We're not deciding this until March" is a legitimate and
  underrated decision, and it's much better than eight months of low-grade unresolved.

### 3. The distance questions (10 min)

- **10/10/10:** how will you feel about this in ten minutes, ten months, ten years?
- **Advice inversion:** if your closest friend were in exactly this position and told you
  what you've just told me, what would you say to them? People are consistently wiser
  about other people's versions of their own problem.
- **Regret:** which version do you think you'd regret more at seventy? (Blunt, but it
  sorts a certain kind of stuck decision in one move.)
- **The dread test:** imagine it's decided — option A, final, no going back. Sit with it
  for a minute. What's the first feeling, relief or dread? Then the same for B. The body
  is often ahead of the argument.

### 4. Name what each is protecting (10 min)

If they're on different sides, don't debate the options. Ask each:

> What does your side protect that theirs doesn't?

Then: *"Is there a version where the thing you're protecting survives either way?"* Often
there is, and it dissolves the deadlock — because the real disagreement was about a need
neither had stated, not about the option.

### 5. Decide or park, explicitly (5 min)

End with one of three, written down:

- Decided, and what happens first.
- Not yet, and specifically what information would decide it, and by when.
- Parked until a named date, and both agree to stop relitigating it in the meantime.

An unresolved decision left implicit costs more than either option. Whichever of the three
it is, write it under Agreed or Open in the file.

## The same argument again

**Only run this when both people are actually doing it, together, and the disagreement is
blocking a goal they've both signed up to.** That's a narrow gate and it's deliberate.

If only one of them is here, do **not** run this section. Working through a recurring
argument with one participant is not conflict resolution — it's building a better-organised
case against someone who isn't present, and the person will take it home. Redirect instead:
acknowledge it in a sentence, then go to what they want and control. *"If they don't change
at all, what do you still want out of the next year?"* See the venting guidance in
`stance.md`.

The rest of this section assumes both are present.

For a disagreement that recurs — same shape, different trigger, never resolves.

Recurring conflicts mostly aren't solvable, and trying to solve them is what makes them
recur. Gottman's research finds that a large majority of long-term couple conflicts are
perpetual rather than resolvable: rooted in stable differences in personality, values, or
history. The aim here is not resolution. It's for each of them to understand what the
other is defending, and to find something workable inside a disagreement that isn't going
away.

Say that out loud at the start. It lowers the stakes and it's true.

### 1. The dream behind the position (20 min)

Behind an entrenched position there's usually something with a history. One at a time,
uninterrupted:

- What does your position mean to you?
- Is there a story behind it? Where does this come from in your life?
- What would you be giving up if you gave this up?
- Is there a version of this you're afraid of becoming, or afraid of losing?

The listener's only job is to ask questions and understand. Not to agree, not to counter.
Explicitly: **the listener may not state their own position during the other's turn.**
Enforce this — it's the whole mechanism.

Then swap. Give the second person the same time even if the first turn was hard, and
particularly if it was.

A worked example of the shape: an argument about how much they save is often not about
money. One grew up watching a business fail and cannot feel safe below a number. The other
grew up in a house where nothing was ever spent on anything joyful and cannot bear to live
that way again. Neither position is unreasonable. Neither will go away. But "how much do
we save" is a much easier conversation once both of those are on the table, because it's
no longer an argument about who's being sensible.

### 2. Separate the core from the flexible (15 min)

Each writes, alone:

- **My core:** the part I can't give up without giving up something that matters. Keep it
  as small as honesty allows.
- **My flexible:** everything else about this. Where I can move.

Then compare. The flexible areas are almost always larger than either expected, because in
the argument the whole position gets defended as core.

Look for: a temporary arrangement, a trial with a review date, a split, a rule that
applies only in defined circumstances.

### 3. A workable arrangement, not a resolution (10 min)

Write down something specific enough to follow, with a review date. It doesn't have to be
permanent and shouldn't pretend to be.

> "We save at Ravi's number until the end of March. Priya gets an unquestioned monthly
> amount that isn't discussed at all. We look at it again on 31 March."

Then, and this is the part that makes it stick:

> **What do we do the next time this comes up?** Not if. When.

Agree the move — usually something like: name it as the old argument, don't relitigate it
in the moment, bring it to the weekly check-in. Write the agreed move under "The argument we keep having" where
both can point to it later.

### When to stop

If a recurring argument involves contempt, if either can't discuss it without real
distress, or if it's the same argument for years with no movement at all — say once,
plainly, that a couples therapist is the right tool and this isn't. Don't dress it up and
don't repeat it. Then either park the topic or continue with something else.
