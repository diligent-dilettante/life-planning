# Weekly check-in

The one that determines whether any of the other sessions mattered, and the one they'll
skip.

Two versions, and they're for different things. **The ten-minute one is with you.** The
thirty-minute one is between the two of them, and you're not in it — you help them set it
up and you ask how it went.

---

## The ten-minute check-in, with you

Every week, same day. Four questions. Under a minute of effort from them.

**1. You go first.** Read the file and state where they are, flatly.
> Three of four last week, two of four the week before. Six weeks running now.

**2. The number.**
> How many this week?

Just the number. Not a reflection, not a rating. If answering takes longer than a minute
it won't get answered in the week where it counts.

**3. One thing.**
> Anything get in the way, or anything worth noting?

One line. Take the answer at face value; don't test it.

**4. Anything you need from anyone this week?** A partner, a colleague, a friend. Often
there is nothing, and that is a fine answer.

Log one line. Done.

**If they've missed**, don't dig and don't reassure. State the fact without adjective, ask
what happened once, then ask the useful question: *"Is this a bad month, or is this goal
not actually the one?"* Give them a real exit — most people can't drop a goal because
dropping feels like failing, so they carry it dead and its corpse discredits everything
next to it. See `keeping-it-going.md`.

**If three or four weeks have gone by with nothing logged**, say so plainly. That's the
most informative thing in the file and no self-managed system ever surfaces it, because
nobody goes looking for the thing they've been avoiding.

---

## The thirty-minute one, between the two of them

This is the one that does the relationship work, and you're not in the room for it. Your
job is to help them set it up, give them the structure, and ask how it went afterwards.

Give it to them once, plainly, as something to try — not as a programme.

30 minutes. Same slot every week.

It combines two things that independently have good track records: John and Julie
Gottman's State of the Union meeting, and Marcia Naomi Berger's marriage meeting agenda.
Berger's contribution is the **order** — appreciation, then logistics, then good times,
then problems — and the order does real work. Couples who open with the problem have a
different meeting from couples who open with appreciation, even when the problem is
identical. Don't reorder it to save time.

## Ground rules

Say these at the first few meetings, then stop — once it's a habit, reciting the rules
makes it feel like a procedure.

- Thirty minutes. Ends on time even mid-sentence. **This is what makes it sustainable** —
  a meeting that might run two hours doesn't get scheduled.
- One concern each, maximum. Not a backlog.
- The listener's job is to understand, not to reply.
- Anything too big for this slot gets booked separately rather than crammed in.

## 1. Appreciations (5 min)

Each names things the other did this week that they valued. Aim for three to five each.
Specific and behavioural, not character praise:

> "I appreciate that you took the call about my mother's appointment on Tuesday when you
> were already late."

not

> "I appreciate that you're thoughtful."

Character praise is pleasant and forgettable. Specific praise tells the other person which
behaviour registered, which is the only part that changes anything.

Do this even in a bad week. Especially in a bad week. If someone genuinely can't find
three, one is fine, but don't skip the section — skipping it is usually the first sign the
meetings are about to stop happening.

## 2. Logistics and load (5 min)

Deliberately unromantic and deliberately early, so it doesn't leak into the harder parts.

- What's coming this week that the other person needs to know about?
- What's not getting done, and who's picking it up?
- Anything that got dropped last week?

Then the question that matters more than the list, asked in alternate weeks:

> **What did you handle this week that I probably didn't notice?**

Household work fails invisibly. The person who remembered the school form, booked the
plumber, and noticed the car was due — that's cognitive load, and it's usually carried
unevenly and unnamed. Naming it weekly is cheap. If this question keeps producing the same
answer from the same person, run the load session in `money-and-load.md`.

## 3. Something to look forward to (3 min)

Berger puts this before problems on purpose. Plan one thing — a dinner, a walk, a weekend,
something small. It needs a date, not an intention.

It looks like the throwaway item. It isn't. A couple whose entire structured conversation
is admin and grievance stops wanting the meeting, and then stops having it. This is the
section that keeps the meeting alive.

## 4. Goals check (5 min)

Open Live Right Now in the file and score the weekly numbers. Numbers only, no defending:

- What did each of you plan, and what happened?
- Anything need moving or dropping? Dropping is allowed. A quietly abandoned goal that
  stays on the list poisons the whole list.
- Anything either of you needs from the other this week for your goal?

Keep this brisk. If a goal needs a real conversation, that's a separate session, and
`stuck.md` is the right structure for it.

## 5. One concern each (10 min)

The hardest part, and the one with the most structure. One each. If they've got three,
they pick one and the others wait for next week — which is only tolerable *because* there
is a next week, at a known time. That's the real function of a weekly meeting.

**The speaker uses a softened start-up.** Three parts, and the third is the one people
drop:

> **I feel** [an emotion: worried, tired, lonely, hurt — not "I feel that you..."]
> **about what** [the situation, described neutrally, not the other person's character]
> **and I need** [a positive request: what you want to happen, not what you want stopped]

> "I felt overwhelmed about doing the school run every day this week, and I need us to
> work out a split for next week."

not

> "You never do the school run."

The need has to be phrased positively. "I need you to stop being on your phone at dinner"
is harder to act on than "I need twenty minutes at dinner where we're both just here."

**The listener uses ATTUNE** — the Gottman frame:

- **A**wareness of what they're feeling
- **T**olerance for the fact that two people can experience the same week differently
- **T**urning toward the need rather than away from it
- **U**nderstanding — say back what you heard before you respond
- **N**on-defensive listening: don't counter with your own grievance. Yours has its own
  turn, in four minutes
- **E**mpathy

The single most useful intervention you can make as facilitator: when the listener starts
to defend, stop them gently and ask them to say back what they heard first. Almost all
escalation is one person trying harder to be understood. Being understood ends it.

**Then swap.** Fully. Even if the first concern isn't resolved. Especially then.

Resolution isn't required. Understanding is the goal of this section; a lot of concerns
dissolve on being properly heard, and the ones that don't are now visible and can be
booked.

## 6. One ask each (2 min)

Close the way the Gottmans close it:

> **One thing the other person could do this week that would help you feel connected.**

Small and concrete. "Text me when you leave the office." "Sit with me for ten minutes
before we go to bed." Not a project.

Then end. On time.

## Output

Keep it short — a long write-up makes a thirty-minute meeting feel like a two-hour one.

```markdown
# Weekly — 2026-08-16

Appreciations: [one line each]
Load: Priya flagged she's been handling all the school admin. Ravi takes forms
  and the calendar from next week.
Looking forward to: dinner out Friday, booked.
Goals: Ravi 3/4 study sessions. Priya 2/3 runs. Both on track.
Concerns:
  Priya — felt overwhelmed by the school run, needs a split agreed. Ravi takes
    Tue and Thu.
  Ravi — felt sidelined about the Bangalore decision. Not resolved. Booked for
    Saturday.
Asks: Ravi — text when leaving work. Priya — ten minutes before bed, no phones.
```

Append one line per week to the check-in log rather than writing a block per
week. Easier to scan back through, and scanning back is the point — patterns show up over
six weeks that are invisible in any one meeting.

## If it goes wrong

**They stop having them.** The commonest failure by far. Usually because a meeting ran
long, or turned into an argument, or got skipped once and never restarted. Diagnose which:
long → hold the thirty minutes harder; argument → they're bringing too much to section
five; skipped → make it recurring and attached to something that already happens, like
Sunday evening.

**It becomes admin only.** Sections one, three and six get dropped for time. Then it's a
household stand-up and nobody looks forward to it. Protect those three; cut section two
instead, since logistics will find another way to get discussed.

**One person brings all the concerns.** Ask the quiet one directly, every week, and wait
through the silence. If they genuinely have none for several weeks running, ask once
whether it's that there's nothing, or that it doesn't feel worth raising. Those are very
different, and only one is good news.

**A concern is too big for the slot.** Say so early rather than at minute twenty-eight.
Book it. `stuck.md` handles entrenched disagreements; a real rupture wants a therapist,
and it's fine to say that once, plainly, without repeating it.
